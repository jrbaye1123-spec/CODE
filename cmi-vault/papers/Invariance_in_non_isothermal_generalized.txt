Invariance in non-isothermal generalized plasticity

Received: 15 January 2013 / Revised: 25 July 2013 / Published online: 9 September 2014
© Springer-Verlag Wien 2014

Abstract Theoretical issues related to the covariant structure of non-isothermal generalized plasticity in large
deformations are studied in depth. For this purpose, the tensor analysis on manifolds is utilized and the manifold
structure of both the ambient and the state spaces is postulated. Building on this, a covariant structure of the
theory is constructed within a strain–space formulation. Thermomechanical loading criteria in both Lagrangian
and Eulerian descriptions are derived in a deformation–temperature space. Based on geometrical concepts,
classical non-isothermal plasticity is derived as a special case. The invariance properties of the local form of
the balance of energy equation are systematically engaged for the derivation of the thermomechanical state
equations. It is specifically shown that the local form of the covariant balance of energy equation does not
yield the Doyle–Ericksen formula and the standard entropy–temperature constitutive relation, unless a further
assumption is made. A derivation of the spatial form of the Duhamel–Neumann hypothesis on a decomposition
of the rate of deformation tensor into its mechanical and thermal parts is performed as well. The covariance
concept is specialized further by the construction of a material model within the context of classical metal
plasticity. The proposed model is tested numerically for the solution of two problems of non-isothermal largescale plastic flow.

1 Introduction
In a rather recent publication (Panoskaltsis et al. [1]), rate-independent generalized plasticity (Lubliner [2–
6]) was revisited and it was placed in a covariant framework. In particular, a stress–space formulation of
the theory was initially derived within a Lagrangian setting and then it was extended to a covariant one by
applying some concepts and results from the tensor analysis on manifolds (e.g., Bishop and Goldberg [7];
Abraham et al. [8]; Lovelock and Rund [9]). The covariance concept was emphasized further by developing
a model within the context of the multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient (e.g., Lee [10];
Mandel [11]; Lubliner [5,6]; Simo [12]; Le and Stumpf [13]; Epstein and Maugin [14]; Cermelli et al. [15];
Gurtin and Anand [16]) and by implementing it numerically, by means of an Eulerian time integration algorithm.
Nevertheless, since the formulation was developed in stress space, it presupposed stability under stress control
and it was therefore limited to work hardening materials. Moreover, no reference was made to the invariance
properties of the energy balance equation, which constitutes the keystone for any covariant theory, relativistic or
nonrelativistic. In a further publication, Panoskaltsis et al. [17] addressed those issues, and by introducing for the

Our motivation for this paper is to proceed further and extend our previous study in cases in which both the
kinematics of the problem and the internal variables are left by the theory entirely unspecified. In particular,
in the present publication, we present a covariant treatment of rate-independent generalized plasticity within
a non-isothermal framework. Furthermore, in this work, we find a decomposition of the Lie derivative of
the Kirchhoff stress tensor into mechanical and thermal parts. Based on this, we provide a derivation of a
decomposition of the rate of deformation tensor into its mechanical and thermal parts in a spatial form (this is
the so-called the Duhamel–Neumann hypothesis).
This study is organized as follows: In Sect. 2, non-isothermal generalized plasticity, which had been
originally proposed in a material setting within the context of set theory and topology (see Lubliner [6]), is
presented within the context of tensor analysis on manifolds. This context, besides allowing a straightforward
derivation of the governing equations in the spatial description, provides the necessary mathematical tools for
a covariant treatment of the theory. In Sect. 3, we revisit our previous approach in Panoskaltsis et al. [17], and
on the basis of a purely geometrical argument, namely the invariance of a set under the action of the flow of a
vector field (e.g., Abraham et al. [8, pp. 256–258]; Brezis [20]), we derive classical plasticity as a special case
of generalized plasticity in the case of general thermomechanical loadings. Generalized plasticity is rendered
fully covariant in Sect. 4, where the invariance properties of the local form of the balance of energy equation,
under the superposition of spatial diffeomorphisms which also include a temperature rescaling, are studied. The
study shows that within the context of non-isothermal generalized plasticity, the covariant balance of energy
does not yield the Doyle–Ericksen formula (Doyle and Ericksen [21]) and the standard entropy–temperature
constitutive equation, unless a further assumption is made. As a next step, a rational derivation of the spatial
form of the Duhamel–Neumann hypothesis concerning a decomposition of the rate of deformation tensor into
its mechanical and thermal parts (see Marsden and Hughes [22, pp. 204–206]) for an elastic–plastic material
is performed in Sect. 5. In Sect. 6, as an application of the covariance concept, a material model is developed
within a fully thermomechanical framework. Computational aspects regarding the coupled thermomechanical
problem and the predictions of the model for two problems of large-scale plastic flow are discussed in Sect. 7.

A local process is defined as elastic if it lies entirely in a seven-dimensional submanifold of D –the
deformation–temperature space–defined by Q = constant, otherwise is defined as plastic. The basic concept
of generalized plasticity is that of the elastic range of a state (e.g., Rivlin and Pipkin [31]; Lucchesi and
Podio-Guidugli [32]; Bertram [33]). This is defined as the manifold comprising the values of (C, T) that can be
reached from the current deformation–temperature state by an elastic process and is a submanifold of the state
space. It is assumed further that the boundary of the elastic range is also a manifold, the points of which have
coordinate neighborhood on it, which is attached to the interior in much the same way the surface of a solid
is attached to the interior. The latter manifold may be defined as a loading surface at Q, (e.g., Eisenberg and
Phillips [34], Lubliner [3,6]). A state within the elastic range may be defined as plastic if it lies on a loading
surface and elastic otherwise.

3 Classical non-isothermal plasticity as a special case
From the aforementioned presentation, it is concluded that the theory does not employ the concept of the
yield surface as a basic ingredient. Nevertheless, the theory does not preclude the existence of a yield surface
(Lubliner [6]), while as it has been shown in Panoskaltsis et al. [17] by considering the yield surface concept and
an invariance argument, classical plasticity may be derived as a special case. The basic objective of this section
is to revisit our previous approach in Panoskaltsis et al. [17] and generalize it under unspecified kinematics
and non-isothermal processes.
A yield hypersurface exists in the state space if the function  [see Eq. (12)] vanishes in a nonvanishing
submanifold of the state space D , which is called the elastic domain. The boundary of the elastic domain may
be considered as a submanifold of one dimension less and constitutes the yield hypersurface. Furthermore,
the projection of the elastic domain onto a manifold Q = constant is defined as the elastic domain at Q and its
boundary as the yield surface. It can be shown (Lubliner [6]) that the elastic domain at Q is a subset of the
elastic range, and accordingly the yield surface may be identified by the initial loading surface (Eisenberg and
Phillips [34]). A particular case of interest arises when the function  is a nonvanishing function. If this is the
case, there are no elastic states, and the elastic domain degenerates to a single surface, which may be called a
quasi-yield surface (Lubliner [3]; Panoskaltsis et al. [1]).
On the other hand, classical plasticity corresponds to the particular case in which the elastic range is
invariant under the action of plastic flow. If that is the case, the closure of the elastic domain (at Q) coincides

4 Thermomechanical state equations
The concept of invariance plays a fundamental role in several branches of mechanics and physics. In particular,
within the context of continuum mechanics, the invariance properties of the energy balance equation under
some groups of transformations may be systematically used in order to derive the conservation laws, the
balance laws, and/or to determine some restrictions related to the equations underlying the material constitutive
response (e.g., Ericksen [40]; Green and Rivlin [41]; Marsden and Hughes [22, pp. 163–167, 200–203]; Simo
and Marsden [30]; S̆ilhavý [42]; Yavari et al. [24]; Kanso et al. [43]; Yavari and Marsden [44]; Yavari [45];
Panoskaltsis et al. [7,17]). For instance, Marsden and Hughes [22, pp. 202–203] by studying the invariant
properties of the local form of the material balance of energy equation, under the superposition of arbitrary
spatial diffeomorphisms which included also a temperature rescaling, proposed a non-isothermal covariant
treatment of nonlinear elasticity. The basic objective of this section is to revisit the approach given by Marsden
and Hughes [22, pp. 202–203], within the context of the rate-independent continuum that obeys the rate Eq. (13)
[or equivalently Eq. (12)].
The central concept of a covariant constitutive theory (see Marsden and Hughes [22, pp. 199–204]) is the
local form of the balance of energy equation and the postulate of its invariance under certain transformations.
Then, by introducing reasonable covariance assumptions for the transformation laws of the thermal variables,
and by operating on the governing equations, one can derive the material thermomechanical state equations.
Within this section, we will find restrictions on the constitutive equations defining the response of a rateindependent continuum. In particular, since the deformation and the temperature are the primary variables,
we will show how the stress and the internal entropy can be derived from the Helmholtz free energy. It is
emphasized that by this approach any reference to the second law of thermodynamics, which constitutes a
basic constitutive ingredient of classical formulations of non-isothermal plasticity (e.g., see Lubliner [2,6];
Mandel [11]; Lehmann [46]; Simo and Miehe [47]; Le and Stumpf [13]; Epstein and Maugin [14]; Acharya
and Shawki [58]) among others), can be entirely bypassed, even in a non-conservative setting.

To this end, it is emphasized that in the present approach the internal variable vector under the superposition
of the diffeomorphism in question does not follow its own mode of evolution [see Eq. (25)], since it is always
related to the control variables by the rate Eq. (13). Accordingly, the rate of the internal variable vector q is
determined in the primed configuration by substitution of the (new) values of g and T, namely g′ = ξ ∗ g and
T ′ = δT , respectively. In this sense, the proposed approach differs vastly from other approaches found within
the context of materials with internal structure (e.g., see Yavari and Marsden [44]), in which an independent
manifold structure is attributed to the material substructure and arbitrary transformations may be superposed
onto it. In these approaches, several balance laws may be derived that are related to the material substructure.
Within the present approach, such a balance law is assumed to be known a priori, and this law is the (local)
rate equation for the evolution of the internal variables. Accordingly, such an equation may be identified by
a balance-like equation between the macroscopic actions (g, T) and the material substructure, as the latter is
described by the internal variable vector q. Further considerations on this subtle point within an isothermal
formulation can be found in our recent work in Panoskaltsis et al. [27]. Related papers are also those of Cermelli
et al. [15] and Gurtin and Anand [16].
Furthermore, within the theory of thermodynamics with internal variables, Lubliner [2,6] has arrived
at a similar result by working entirely in the reference configuration and on the basis of the second law of
thermodynamics expressed in the form of the Clausius–Planck inequality, which is a stronger (i.e., less general)
form of the Clausius–Duhem inequality, since it ignores dissipation due to heat conduction. In order to obtain
the standard thermomechanical state equations, Lubliner modifies further the Clausius–Planck inequality,
by assuming that it holds as an equality for elastic unloading and neutral loading (see also Acharya and
Shawki [58]). It is remarkable to note that by working with the covariance axiom, we do not have to ignore
dissipation due to heat conduction. Also, in comparing the two approaches, we note that while in the second
law of thermodynamics we focus on all processes, in the covariance axiom we focus on all transformations of
a given process (Marsden and Hughes [22, p. 201]).

developed primarily in the reference configuration, and the derivation of thermomechanical state equations,
which was performed entirely in the spatial configuration, the constitutive model is derived in the relaxed space.
Thus, (see Panoskaltsis et al. [1]), the relaxed space, as well as any other configuration of the body, can serve
as a reference configuration, too. We remark at this point that this clearly explains the subtle notion of multiple
reference configurations. Accordingly, as it is noted further in Panoskaltsis et al. [1]: “…, all configurations
of the body are practically indistinguishable and the equation forming is a matter of observation”. A similar
comment has been also made, within the context of nonlinear elasticity, by Sansour [48]. In particular, Sansour
[48] states: “In our treatment, we avoid the use of phrases such as ‘material tensors’ or ‘spatial tensors’ since
we believe that such tensors do not exist. Instead, we believe that the kind of description one is adopting to
describe a deformation process is completely independent of any stress or strain tensors used, the latter being
themselves physically interesting objects that exist whether the deformation is described in a material or in
a spatial form. Nevertheless, the use of a certain stress or strain tensor can be suitable (or not) for a certain
kind of description, in the sense that the formulation of the physical process in terms of these tensors takes
the most simple possible form.” Both statements can be considered as two parallel interpretations within the
context of the mechanics of deformable bodies of the following Dyson’s (Dyson [49]) comment: “Einstein
based his theory of general relativity on the principle that God did not attach any preferred labels to the
points of space-time.” Further, it is emphasized that by noting that the convected Lie derivative in the reference
configuration is degenerated into the ordinary time derivative, the material rate Eq. (12) are identical to the
spatial rate Eq. (13), with the deformation gradient in the former of them being equal to the (two-point) unit
tensor I. As a result, once the equations describing the state of the body are known in some configuration, they
are also known in any configuration by employing the covariant transformation laws.

7 Computational aspects and numerical simulations
7.1 Computational implications
In general, the numerical treatment of coupled thermomechanical problems is performed on the basis of
a split of the governing equations (equations of motion, constitutive equations, energy balance equation,
and appropriate boundary conditions) into mechanical and thermal parts. For instance, on the basis of an
isothermal split proposed in the pioneering work of Simo and Miehe [47], several constitutive models have been
proposed and implemented within the finite element context, e.g., Canadija and Brnic [50], where temperaturedependent material parameters are considered, and Hakansson et al. [51], where kinematic hardening effects
are taken into account. However, this rather straightforward numerical scheme has the disadvantage of not
being unconditionally stable. An alternative time integration algorithm relies on an adiabatic split (see Armero
and Simo [52]), in which the total entropy is held constant, followed by a thermal phase at fixed configuration,
which leads to an unconditionally stable algorithm. This algorithm has been developed further in order to
deal with materials with phase changes, which can also include frictional contact, by Agelet de Saracibar
et al. [53,54].
Nevertheless, since our main objective is to develop generalized plasticity in a covariant framework (under
arbitrary deformations), we consider two rather simple problems of adiabatic overheating, in which the equations of motion and the boundary conditions are trivially satisfied. Moreover, due to the dynamic rates resulting
in adiabatic response, heat loss due to conduction, convection, and radiation can be neglected in comparison with
the material self-heating induced by plastic dissipation, which leads to thermomechanical processes that can
be considered as homogeneous. Such processes include the elastic–plastic uniaxial compression experiments
in a Kolsky pressure bar reported by Rosakis et al. [55]. Accordingly, within our simulations, a simultaneous solution of the remaining governing equations, namely the constitutive equations and the energy balance
equation, is performed.
The time integration procedure may in principle be formulated equivalently with respect to the reference,
relaxed or the current configurations. Since we deal with large-scale plastic flow, the kinematics of the problem
together with the principle of covariance suggest that a numerical formulation in terms of the spatial Kirchhoff
stress and its convected derivative is more fundamental. Further, in the current configuration, the spatial metric
usually has a diagonal form, which makes the computations simpler than those in the current or the relaxed
configurations where the metrics C and Ce are fully populated (e.g., Miehe [26]; Panoskaltsis et al. [1]). For
this purpose, the model basic equations [see Eqs. (56), (63), (64), (67), (68)] are written in their spatial form

To this end, it is noted that when the deformation gradient F is known, the thermomechanical state Eq. (76),
and the rate equations for the evolution of the internal variables [Eqs. (77), (78), (79)] together with the
temperature evolution Eq. (81) form a system of five equations in the five unknowns τ, be , α, ηp and T . This
system can be solved by time discretizing it by a backward Euler scheme within the framework of an (elastic)
predictor–(plastic) corrector scheme. In sharp contrast to the classical elastic–plastic case (see Panoskaltsis
et al. [1,56]), in a generalized plasticity-based model, the state variables are not constrained to lie within
the manifold enclosed by the yield surface, because of the absence of the yield surface concept. Accordingly,
unlike the classical elastic–plastic case where the governing equations define a unilaterally constrained problem
of evolution (e.g., Simo and Miehe [47]), in the present case the governing equations define a differential
system, which must obey the non-isothermal loading–unloading criteria (82). The resulting system is, in
general, nonlinear, and it can be solved by an iterative technique in multi-dimensions. Algorithmic aspects
related to the crucial role played by the loading–unloading criteria (82) for the numerical implementation of a
generalized plasticity model, together with a general method for approximating the Lie derivatives appearing
in Eqs. (76), (77), and (78), can be found in Panoskaltsis et al. [1,56].

8 Concluding remarks
A new covariant formulation of the constitutive theory of non-isothermal generalized plasticity in finite deformations has been developed within a deformation (strain) space setting.
The basic ingredients of this formulation are as follows:
1. A manifold underlying structure that accounts not only for the body of interest and the ambient space,
but for the state space as well. Accordingly, the state space is considered as a fiber over the material
point, a procedure leading to a local vector bundle mapping, which is used for the identification of the
thermodynamical process under examination.
2. The postulate of a local form of the spatial covariant balance of energy and the examination of its invariance
properties under the superposition of arbitrary spatial diffeomorphisms, which include also a temperature
rescaling.
Like in an isothermal case, studied in Panoskaltsis et al. [17], it is shown that the covariant balance of energy
does not yield the Doyle–Ericksen formula and the standard entropy–temperature relationship unless a further

952

V. P. Panoskaltsis et al.

Fig. 9 Plane strain: temperature evolution versus elongation λ

assumption is made, namely that the unloading from a plastic state be quasi-reversible, that is, that in such a
process the plastic dissipation vanishes. It is noteworthy that this result is in full agreement with a result derived
by means of the Clausius–Planck inequality, which is a particular case of the second law of thermodynamics
expressed by means of the Clausius–Duhem inequality, by Lubliner (Lubliner [2,6]). It is noted however that
our approach is more general, since in our approach the restrictive assumption of neglecting dissipation due
to heat conduction, assumed in the Clausius–Planck inequality, is not used.
Furthermore, in the course of the development, several novel features underlying the mathematical structure
of rate-independent plasticity are presented. These novel features are as follows:
(i) A derivation of classical plasticity as a special case of generalized plasticity within a non-isothermal
framework on the basis of the flow invariance theorem of Brezis (Brezis [20]), with the consistency
condition identified as the invariance condition of the state space under the action of plastic flow.
(ii) A proof of a decomposition of the Lie derivative of the Kirchhoff stress tensor into mechanical and
thermal parts, for an elastic–plastic material under arbitrary deformations. Moreover, a derivation of the
Duhamel–Neumann hypothesis for the same material and under the same conditions. It is shown that in
this case the decomposition of the rate of deformation tensor into mechanical and thermal parts holds,
but now the material compliance tensors have some extra terms in them. These terms are related directly
to the plastic properties of the material, as these properties are described by the internal variables and
their evolution, in the course of the thermal–plastic loading.
(iii) The derivation of a new generalized plasticity constitutive model within a fully thermomechanical
framework, which for the particular case of classical non-isothermal plasticity has as a limit the model
considered by Simo and Miehe [47].
(iv) A novel flow rule in terms of the metric of the relaxed space, which yields a flow rule in terms of the
symmetric part of the plastic velocity gradient, like the one derived by Lubliner [6] (see also Simo [12];
Simo and Miehe [47]) on the basis of the maximum plastic dissipation postulate, only in the particular
case of a Euclidean relaxed space.
Computational aspects and numerical simulations showing some unique predictive capabilities of generalized
plasticity, which are not shared by the classical theory, have been also presented.
