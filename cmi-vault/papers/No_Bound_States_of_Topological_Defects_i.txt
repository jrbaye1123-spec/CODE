No Bound States of Topological Defects
in Overdamped Active Media
A No-Go Theorem, and the Inertial Threshold that Evades It
Dennis Norman Brown
August 2026

Abstract
A self-propelled + 12 disclination orbiting its − 21 partner, sustained by metabolism and continuously missing collision, is the natural hypothesis for a persistent defect pair in active matter.
It is false, and the obstruction is structural rather than parametric.
We reduce the two-defect problem exactly to a two-dimensional flow on the cylinder
(0, ∞) × S 1 in the separation d and the angle θ between propulsion and separation (Theorem 5). Reflection symmetry about the separation axis makes θ ∈ {0, π} invariant for any
reflection-antisymmetric reorientation law, so the cylinder splits into two non-communicating
strips and no winding orbit can exist (Theorem 7).
Within a strip we exhibit the Dulac function B = 1/ sin θ, for which

Remark 1 (Scope). This is a theoretical study of a dynamical obstruction. It contains no clinical
content. The results constrain any model of defect binding in an overdamped active medium, in
tissue or otherwise.

1

Introduction

1.1

The temptation

Topological defects in active nematics are self-propelled. A + 21 disclination carries a polar direction p set by the local director field and moves along it at a speed proportional to the activity;
its − 21 partner, being three-fold symmetric, does not self-propel. The two attract elastically as
1/d.
1

Set beside the two-body problem of celestial mechanics, the analogy is immediate and seductive. A body attracted as an inverse power, given a transverse velocity, orbits. If a + 12 defect
is continuously propelled transverse to its separation from the − 12 partner, why should it not
orbit indefinitely, sustained by metabolism, never colliding? Such a configuration would furnish
exactly what theories of persistent tissue states require: a defect pair that neither annihilates nor
disperses, holding a topological configuration for as long as the tissue is fed.
The analogy fails, and it fails for a reason that no choice of parameters can repair.

1.2

Why the celestial intuition misleads

Binary stars orbit because gravity acts on massive bodies that carry momentum through the
transverse direction. Defects in an overdamped medium are massless in the dynamical sense: at
Reynolds number ∼10−5 the inertial term is absent, not merely small. The equation of motion is
ṙ = µF

rather than

(1)

so velocity is slaved instantaneously to force and the trajectory follows the instantaneous force
direction at every instant. A closed orbit requires transverse velocity to persist while the radial
force acts, which is a statement about inertia. With m = 0 that persistence is unavailable.
That paragraph is a physical argument, not a proof. It is also incomplete: it addresses periodic
orbits and says nothing about the possibility of a stationary bound pair at fixed separation — a
circular orbit — which requires no oscillation at all and is not obviously excluded by the absence
of momentum. The purpose of this paper is to replace the argument with theorems, to close
the gap it leaves, and to determine exactly how much inertia would be needed to overturn the
conclusion.

1.3

Results

R1. Exact reduction. Translation and rotation invariance reduce the two-defect problem to
a planar flow on a cylinder in (d, θ) (Theorem 5). Two-dimensionality is what makes the
problem decidable.
R2. Invariant axis. Reflection symmetry makes θ ∈ {0, π} invariant for any admissible
reorientation law, forbidding winding orbits (Theorem 7).
R3. Area expansion. The Dulac function B = 1/ sin θ makes the flow strictly area-expanding,
forbidding every attractor at finite separation, periodic orbits and stable equilibria alike
(Theorem 9). This is the main result, and it is strictly stronger than a no-orbit statement.
R4. The equilibrium, and its instability. A unique interior equilibrium exists but has
positive trace for all parameters (Theorem 12).
R5. Global classification. Every trajectory annihilates or unbinds (Theorem 14); numerics
confirm with zero exceptions in 1280 runs.
R6. The masquerade law. Apparent revolutions grow only logarithmically in the fineness
of preparation (Theorem 16), which bounds how long an observer must watch.
R7. The inertial threshold. Binding is restored above Stc = 0.19; tissue sits at 10−5 (Theorem 20).

Theorem 14 (Global classification). Under the hypotheses of Theorem 9, every trajectory in
S± satisfies either d → 0 in finite time ( annihilation) or d → ∞ ( unbinding). No trajectory
remains in a compact subset of S± bounded away from d = 0 and d = ∞ for all time, except the
equilibrium itself and its measure-zero stable set.
Proof. Suppose a forward trajectory remains in a compact K ⊂ S+ with 0 < dmin ≤ d ≤ dmax <
∞. Its ω-limit set is non-empty, compact, connected and invariant. By Poincaré–Bendixson
in the plane, ω is an equilibrium, a closed orbit, or a union of equilibria and connecting orbits.
Closed orbits are excluded by Theorem 9(i). The only equilibrium is that of Theorem 12, which is
unstable with a two-dimensional unstable manifold when det J > 0 and tr J > 0; hence it attracts
only its stable set, which is empty for a source and at most one-dimensional in the saddle-free
non-spiral case, in either case of measure zero. Homoclinic loops would bound a region and are
excluded by the same Dulac argument. Hence ω is empty for almost every initial condition,
contradicting compactness, so K cannot confine the trajectory.

Corollary 18 (Observational threshold). To sustain N apparent revolutions the preparation must
be exponentially fine, δ ∼ e−2πσN/ω . Equivalently, an observer who has watched a candidate bound
pair for a time T can conclude binding only if T ≫ 1/σ = 1/ak 2 ; below that, a pseudo-orbit is
the generic expectation, not the exception.
Remark 19 (Why this matters). The no-go theorem says bound pairs do not exist. The masquerade law says pairs that look bound are common and their lifetime is controlled by a single
computable rate. Both are needed: without the second, an observation of ten clean revolutions
would appear to refute the first. It does not — ten revolutions requires only δ ∼ 10−3 , which is
unremarkable preparation.

Stochastic reorientation

Admissibility (Definition 3) assumed deterministic reorientation. Rotational diffusion is the natural stochastic alternative, and it does not rescue binding.
Proposition 23 (No confined stationary state). Let θ additionally undergo rotational diffusion
of strength Dr > 0, so that the process on C has generator L = f ∂d + g∂θ + Dr ∂θ2 . Then no
stationary probability density supported in a compact subset of S± bounded away from d = 0 and
d = ∞ exists.
Proof. Diffusion in θ destroys the invariance of θ ∈ {0, π}, so the two strips communicate; but this
˙ =
only enlarges the accessible set. Consider V (d) = ln d. Along the deterministic flow, V̇ = d/d
−2µF (d)/d − µfa cos θ/d. Averaging over the θ-marginal of any putative stationary density and
using that diffusion drives θ toward uniformity on the region where the drift g is weak, the cos θ
term averages toward zero while the strictly negative term −2µF (d)/d survives, giving LV < 0
on any compact annulus with F bounded below. A strict Lyapunov drift toward d = 0 on every
such annulus is incompatible with a stationary density supported there; the process exits with
probability one.
Remark 24. Proposition 23 is stated for the elastically dominated regime where 2F (d)/d exceeds
the propulsion scale. In the opposite regime the same argument run with V = − ln d gives escape
to large d. The intermediate case is precisely the neighbourhood of the equilibrium of Theorem 12,
which is unstable, so noise accelerates rather than delays departure. We regard the deterministic
results as the substantive ones and this proposition as confirming that noise does not create what
the deterministic flow forbids.

Scope and consequences

What is excluded. Any model in which a ± 12 defect pair in an overdamped active medium
is held at finite separation — by orbiting, by a stationary balance of propulsion against elastic
attraction, or by any attracting configuration whatever — is excluded, for every central attraction
weakening with distance, every mobility, every activity, and every reflection-symmetric reorientation law satisfying (10). No parameter choice evades this.
What is not excluded. The theorem is about a pair in isolation. It says nothing about
(i) pinning by external quenched heterogeneity, which supplies a force not derivable from the
pair coordinates and is therefore outside Definition 2; (ii) many-body configurations, where the
reduction of Theorem 5 fails; (iii) defect lines in three dimensions; (iv) media with inertia above
Stc . Each is a genuine open direction rather than a loophole, and (i) in particular is the mechanism
most likely to be operating wherever persistent defect configurations are actually observed.
Why the reduction is the load-bearing step. The obstruction is dimensional. In two
dimensions Poincaré–Bendixson and Dulac decide the existence of recurrent behaviour outright; in
three or more they do not, and periodic orbits are generic. Theorem 5 is what places the problem
in the decidable class, and it does so only because the interaction is central and the propulsion
enters through a single angle. Any generalisation that breaks either property — noncentral forces, hydrodynamic coupling through the ambient flow, a second propulsive
defect — leaves the decidable class, and the theorem should not be expected to
survive it.

Limitations

 Two dimensions, point defects. Theorem 9 is proved for point defects in a plane. Defect
lines in three dimensions carry additional degrees of freedom and the two-body reduction does
not carry over, though the overdamped argument does.
 The general reorientation case is conditional. Theorem 9 is unconditional for Ω =
ω(d) sin θ. For higher harmonics the criterion (10) must be checked; it holds automatically
near the axis but we have not proved it in the interior for arbitrary Ω.
 Stc is numerical and parameter-dependent. Equation (17) is obtained at one (a, k).
That Stc is O(1) is robust and follows from the two limits in the proof; the value 0.19 is not
universal.
 Proposition 23 is a drift argument, not a full ergodic classification of the diffusion on C.
We state it as confirming rather than establishing.
 Mobility is taken isotropic and constant. A spatially varying mobility would add a term
to (5) and require rechecking (9).

What is claimed. That the two-defect problem reduces exactly to a planar flow (Theorem 5);
that reflection symmetry forbids winding orbits (Theorem 7); that the flow is strictly areaexpanding and therefore admits no attractor at finite separation (Theorem 9); that the unique
interior equilibrium is always unstable (Theorem 12); that every trajectory annihilates or unbinds
(Theorem 14); that pseudo-orbits obey a logarithmic depth–duration law (Theorem 16); and that
inertia restores binding above Stc = O(1), four orders above tissue (Theorem 20).

Provenance

The physical claim that overdamped defect pairs cannot orbit, and the original numerical scan
reporting no persistent trajectories, are due to the author’s earlier treatment of active topological
auto-immunity, where the argument was made on the grounds that overdamped dynamics carries no momentum. The present paper supplies what that argument lacked: the exact reduction
11

(Theorem 5), a proof rather than an appeal to inertia (Theorems 7–9), the extension from “no
orbits” to “no attractors of any kind”, which closes the circular-bound-state gap the earlier argument left open (Theorem 9(ii)), the identification and stability analysis of the interior equilibrium
(Theorem 12), the depth–duration law for pseudo-orbits (Theorem 16), and the inertial threshold
(Theorem 20). The Dulac function (8) and the observation that both terms of θ̇ are proportional
to sin θ are the technical keys and are new here.
