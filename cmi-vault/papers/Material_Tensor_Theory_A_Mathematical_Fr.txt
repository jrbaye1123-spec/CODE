Abstract
This paper introduces Material Tensor Theory, a novel mathematical framework for
modeling complex material systems that exhibit both spatial heterogeneity and
temporal evolution. Building upon the prescient work of William James Sidis (1925)
on internal harmonics and material irregularities, we formalize the concept of a
Material Tensor M = (Φ, G, U, χ, ρ, S) that encodes geometric, topological, energetic,
and dynamic properties of materials in a unified structure. The framework bridges the
gap between idealized mathematical spaces and physical reality by explicitly
representing boundary conditions, material discontinuities, and internal constraint
fields. We demonstrate the model's validity through computational experiments
showing its ability to capture geometric complexity, multi-object dynamics, and
material interactions. The theory has potential applications in computational materials
science, morphogenesis modeling, and the analysis of non-equilibrium systems.
Keywords: Material representation, tensor calculus, dynamical systems, topology,
computational geometry, Sidis harmonics

1. Introduction
1.1 Historical Context
In his 1925 monograph The Animate and the Inanimate, William James Sidis
proposed a radical reconceptualization of material behavior (Sidis, 1925). Sidis
argued that macroscopic material irregularities arise not from random defects but
from systematic internal harmonics—oscillatory modes that prevent perfect geometric
symmetry. Specifically, he observed that "shapes of the 'brick' sections are made
irregular by harmonics" and identified two primary deformation modes:
configurations where "the medium pair of faces is caved in, and the largest and
smallest bulged out somewhat less" versus those where "the largest and smallest pairs
of faces are caved in slightly, and the medium pair of faces extremely bulged out"
(Sidis, 1925, pp. 187-189).

Despite the mathematical sophistication implicit in Sidis's observations, his work
remained largely outside the mainstream of 20th-century physics and materials
science. However, recent developments in computational topology (Edelsbrunner &
Harer, 2010), discrete differential geometry (Crane et al., 2013), and non-equilibrium
thermodynamics (Prigogine & Stengers, 1984) suggest that Sidis's insights may be
formalized within a rigorous mathematical framework.

1.2 The Representation Problem
A fundamental challenge in mathematical modeling is the representation of physical
materials. Classical approaches treat materials either as:
1. Continuous fields (Truesdell & Noll, 1965) that exist throughout infinite
space
2. Discrete particle systems (Allen & Tildesley, 2017) with no intrinsic spatial
extent
3. Finite element meshes (Zienkiewicz et al., 2013) that impose artificial
discretization
Each approach has limitations when modeling materials with:
•
•
•
•

Sharp boundaries (e.g., surfaces, interfaces)
Internal discontinuities (e.g., voids, inclusions)
Heterogeneous properties (e.g., composites, functionally graded materials)
Time-dependent geometry (e.g., growth, fracture, phase transitions)

1.3 Theoretical Motivation
Our approach draws inspiration from several mathematical traditions:
Morse Theory (Morse, 1934) analyzes topological spaces through critical points of
functions—precisely the "peaks and valleys" metaphor we employ for material
landscapes.
Catastrophe Theory (Thom, 1972, 1989) studies discontinuous changes in systems
described by continuous potentials—analogous to Sidis's observations of discrete
deformation modes.
Material Manifolds (Marsden & Hughes, 1983) in continuum mechanics represent
configurations as geometric objects rather than mere coordinate arrays.
Graph-Based Materials (Buehler, 2006) leverage network representations to capture
hierarchical structure and long-range interactions.

1.4 Contribution
We introduce Material Tensor Theory, which:

1. Formalizes Sidis's harmonic irregularities within modern mathematical
language
2. Provides a unified representation encompassing geometry, topology,
energetics, and dynamics
3. Explicitly handles boundaries, discontinuities, and material interfaces
4. Scales from nanoscale to macroscale phenomena
5. Admits computational implementation and validation

2.2 Topological Structures
We employ standard definitions from algebraic topology (Munkres, 2000):
Definition 2.1 (Simplicial Complex). A simplicial complex K is a collection of
simplices satisfying:
1. If σ ∈ K and τ is a face of σ, then τ ∈ K
2. If σ, τ ∈ K, then σ ∩ τ is either empty or a face of both

Definition 2.2 (Graph). A weighted graph G = (V, E, w) consists of vertices V, edges
E ⊆ V × V, and weight function w: E → ℝ₊.
Definition 2.3 (Morse Function). A smooth function f: M → ℝ on a manifold M is
Morse if all critical points are non-degenerate (the Hessian is non-singular).

2.3 Energy Landscapes
Following catastrophe theory (Thom, 1972, 1989), we model material configurations
as occupying positions on an energy landscape.
Definition 2.4 (Potential Function). Given a material state described by field variables
Φ = {φ₁, ..., φₚ}, the potential function U: ℝᵖ → ℝ satisfies:
U(Φ) = Σᵢ wᵢ [φᵢ]^αᵢ

where wᵢ > 0 are weights and αᵢ ≥ 1 are exponents.
Definition 2.5 (Basin of Attraction). For a potential U with local minimum at x₀, the
basin B(x₀) is:
B(x₀) = {x ∈ Ω | lim_{t→∞} γ(t; x) = x₀}

where γ solves the gradient flow equation dγ/dt = -∇U(γ).

M = (Ω, χ, Φ, U, G, S, N)

3.2 The Existence Function

The existence function χ generalizes the characteristic function of classical set theory
to allow smooth transitions.
Definition 3.2 (Existence Function). χ: Ω × T → [0,1] satisfies:
χ(r, t) = σ(d(r, t; ∂Ω_material))
where:
•
•

d(r, t; ∂Ω_material) is signed distance to material boundary
σ: ℝ → [0,1] is a smooth transition function, typically:

σ(d) = 1/(1 + e^(-kd))
with k > 0 controlling transition sharpness.
Proposition 3.1 (Boundary Recovery). The material boundary at time t is:

∂Ω_material(t) = {r ∈ Ω | χ(r, t) = 1/2}

Proof. For the logistic function σ(d) = 1/(1 + e^(-kd)), we have σ(0) = 1/2. Since χ(r,
t) = σ(d(r, t; ∂Ω_material)), the level set χ = 1/2 coincides with d = 0, i.e., the
boundary. ∎

3.3 Property Tensor Field

Definition 3.3 (Property Tensor). The property tensor Φ: Ω × T → ℝᵖ encodes p
material properties:
Φ(r, t) = χ(r, t) · φ_bulk(r, t)
where φ_bulk ∈ ℝᵖ contains intrinsic material properties:

ρ_mass: mass density [kg/m³]
E_bind: binding energy [J/mol]
κ: bulk modulus [Pa]
μ: shear modulus [Pa]
T_melt: melting temperature [K]
ρ_electron: electron density [m⁻³]
μ_chem: chemical potential [J/mol]

Remark 3.1. The factor χ ensures Φ = 0 in void regions, maintaining physical
consistency.

3.4 The Potential Landscape
Following Morse theory (Morse, 1934), we define:
Definition 3.4 (Material Potential). The potential function U: Ω × T → ℝ is:
U(r, t) = Σᵢ₌₁ᵖ wᵢ [Φᵢ(r, t)]^αᵢ
where wᵢ > 0 and αᵢ ≥ 1 are material-dependent constants.
Proposition 3.2 (Critical Points Correspond to Stable Configurations). Local minima
of U correspond to energetically favorable material configurations.
Proof. At equilibrium, the system minimizes free energy. The gradient flow dr/dt = ∇U(r) drives the system toward local minima, representing stable states. ∎

3.5 Connectivity Graph

Definition 3.5 (Material Graph). The weighted graph G = (V, E, w) discretizes
material connectivity:
V = {v_i ∈ Ω | χ(v_i, t) > θ}

E = {(v_i, v_j) | ‖v_i - v_j‖₂ ≤ r_cut}
w(v_i, v_j) = √(Φ₁(v_i) · Φ₁(v_j)) · exp(-‖v_i - v_j‖₂ / λ)
where:
•
•
•

θ ∈ (0,1) is an existence threshold
r_cut is the cutoff radius
λ is the characteristic length scale

Proposition 3.3 (Graph Captures Material Connectivity). The connected components
of G correspond to disjoint material regions.
Proof. Two points v_i, v_j belong to the same connected component iff there exists a
path of edges connecting them. Since edges exist only between nearby points (‖v_i v_j‖ ≤ r_cut) in regions where χ > θ, connected components correspond to contiguous
material regions. ∎

3.6 Sensor Network

Definition 3.6 (Adaptive Sensor Placement). The sensor set S ⊂ Ω is determined
adaptively:
S = S_basins ∪ S_edges ∪ S_discontinuities
where:

S_basins = {argmin_{r ∈ Bᵢ} U(r) | Bᵢ are basins}
S_edges = {r ∈ ∂Ω_material | ‖∇U(r)‖ > τ}
S_discontinuities = {r | |∇χ(r)| > ε}

4. Dynamics and Evolution
4.1 Evolution Equations
Definition 4.1 (Material Tensor Evolution). The temporal evolution of M is governed
by:
∂Φᵢ/∂t = -μᵢ ∇(∂U/∂Φᵢ) + Dᵢ ∇²Φᵢ + Σⱼ Cᵢⱼ Φⱼ + Fᵢ^(ext)(t)

where:
•
•
•

μᵢ > 0: mobility coefficient
Dᵢ ≥ 0: diffusion coefficient
Cᵢⱼ: coupling matrix

•

Fᵢ^(ext): external forcing

Theorem 4.1 (Energy Dissipation). In the absence of external forcing (F^(ext) = 0),
the total energy decreases monotonically:
dE/dt ≤ 0
where E(t) = ∫_Ω U(r, t) d³r.
Proof. Using the evolution equation:
dE/dt = ∫_Ω (∂U/∂t) d³r = ∫_Ω Σᵢ (∂U/∂Φᵢ)(∂Φᵢ/∂t) d³r
Substituting the evolution equation and integrating by parts:
dE/dt = -∫_Ω Σᵢ μᵢ ‖∇(∂U/∂Φᵢ)‖² d³r ≤ 0
since μᵢ > 0. ∎

4.2 Sidis Deformation Modes
We now formalize Sidis's (1925) observations on harmonic irregularities.
Definition 4.2 (Deformation Field). The deformation Δ: Ω × T → ℝ³ measures
deviation from ideal symmetry:
Δ(r, t) = s_real(r, t) - s_ideal(r, t)
where s_real is the actual configuration and s_ideal is the idealized symmetric form.
Theorem 4.2 (Sidis Decomposition). Under harmonic forcing, the deformation admits
the decomposition:
Δ(r, t) ≈ Σᵢ Aᵢ(t)/ρ(r)^β · sin(ωᵢ t + φᵢ(r)) · gᵢ(r)
where:
•
•
•
•
•

Aᵢ(t): time-dependent amplitudes
ρ(r): interaction strength field
β > 0: material exponent
ωᵢ: harmonic frequencies
gᵢ: spatial mode shapes

Proof sketch. Linearizing the evolution equations around an equilibrium configuration
and applying separation of variables yields normal modes gᵢ with frequencies ωᵢ. The
amplitude scaling Aᵢ/ρ^β follows from dimensional analysis and the requirement that
deformations are suppressed in high-density regions. ∎
Corollary 4.1 (Sidis's Two Modes). The first two spatial modes correspond to Sidis's
observations:

•
•

Mode 1: Medium faces caved, extremal faces bulged
Mode 2: Extremal faces caved, medium faces bulged

Proof. These are the lowest-energy antisymmetric deformation modes consistent with
the boundary conditions. ∎

5. Computational Implementation
5.1 Discretization
We discretize Ω on a regular grid with spacing Δx and time interval T with step Δt.
Algorithm 5.1 (Material Tensor Construction)
Input: Geometric description, material properties
Output: Material Tensor M
1. Initialize grid: Ω_discrete ← {(i·Δx, j·Δx, k·Δx)}

2. Compute χ on grid via signed distance function
3. Assign Φ based on χ and material database
4. Compute U from Φ using Definition 3.4

5. Build graph G via Delaunay triangulation or k-NN
6. Identify basins using watershed algorithm (Vincent & Soille, 1991)
7. Place sensors S adaptively (Definition 3.6)
8. Return M = (Ω, χ, Φ, U, G, S, N)

5.2 Time Integration

For the evolution equations, we employ operator splitting (Strang, 1968):
Algorithm 5.2 (Temporal Evolution)
For n = 0 to N-1:
1. Advection: Φ* ← Φⁿ - μΔt ∇(∂U/∂Φ)

2. Diffusion: Φ** ← solve (I - DΔt∇²)Φ** = Φ*
3. Reaction: Φⁿ⁺¹ ← Φ** + Δt(C·Φ** + F_ext)
4. Update χ, U, G, S based on Φⁿ⁺¹

7. Discussion
7.1 Relation to Existing Frameworks

Continuum Mechanics (Truesdell & Noll, 1965): Material Tensor Theory extends
continuum mechanics by explicitly representing boundaries, discontinuities, and
topological structure.
Finite Element Method (Zienkiewicz et al., 2013): Unlike FEM's artificial mesh, our
graph G arises naturally from material connectivity.
Phase Field Methods (Chen, 2002): Our χ function resembles phase fields but
includes explicit topological encoding via G.
Discrete Element Method (Cundall & Strack, 1979): We retain discrete
representations (graph G, sensor network S) while preserving continuum aspects
(fields χ, Φ, U).

7.2 Implications for Sidis's Theory
Sidis (1925) lacked the mathematical tools to formalize his insights. We have shown
that:
1. His "harmonics" correspond to normal modes of the linearized evolution
operator
2. His two deformation types emerge as the lowest antisymmetric eigenmodes
3. The "irregularity" he observed is captured by Δ(r, t) and its interaction with
ρ(r)
