Summary. Two different approaches to modeling evolving anisotropic material behavior are investigated. The
first approach models the evolution of the directionally dependent properties of the material in terms of evolution
laws for the director vectors. In the second approach the evolution law for the plastic velocity gradient is
enhanced by additional terms. It is shown that both approaches lead to an evolution of the substructure that
guides the directionally dependent properties of the material. The two approaches are analyzed within a
thermodynamic setting and the restrictions imposed by the second law of thermodynamics are investigated. The
two approaches are also compared from a kinematic point of view and equivalence between the two approaches
is shown for some specific conditions. To further compare the approaches in the case in which equivalence
cannot be found, two different evolution laws are considered, one for the substructure and the other for the
plastic velocity gradient. These are compared for a loading that corresponds to a plane strain volume preserving
deformation as well as simple shear. Here the Kirchhoff stress and the evolution of the director vectors are
studied during the deformation process.

1 Introduction
The modeling of anisotropic material behavior is still a very active research field today. Even though
this has been an active research field for several decades there are still issues that are not completely
described, one of these being the evolution of anisotropic material behavior at finite elasto-plastic
strains. Describing this phenomenon properly requires profound knowledge of the kinematic
description of a material. In modeling the behavior of anisotropic material one can incorporate
structural variables representing the directionally dependent properties of the material see, e.g. [5],
[23], [31]. The structural variables can be constructed from director vectors which are kinematic
quantities representing the preferred directions of the material. The director vectors can be linked to
such physical quantities as the fibers in fiber-reinforced composites, crystal lattices, or just in a
phenomenological way represent directionally dependent properties.
It is assumed that when the material is exposed to large inelastic deformations the directionally
dependent properties will evolve in some fashion. This evolution has been described and discussed in
the literature in a variety of ways. One approach of this sort is to state evolution laws for the

and Maugin [15], Reese [28], Menzel and Steinmann [27], Menzel [26] and Svendsen et al. [35]. In

thermodynamic considerations into account as well. Another approach used is to add a skewsymmetric component to the plastic velocity gradient, often referred to as the plastic spin, in order to
describe the evolution of the directionally dependent properties. The plastic spin can be interpreted
as the relative rotation of the continuum with respect to the substructure, see, e.g. [8]. If the director
vectors are embedded in the substructure, the plastic spin describes then how the continuum spins
around the director vectors. The plastic spin is given by a constitutive law and will control the
evolution of the directionally dependent properties of the material. This approach has been utilized
by a number of authors, including Loret [24], Dafalias [7]–[9], Stumpf and Badur [33], Van der
Giessen [39], Arravas [1], [2], Kuroda [20], Kuroda and Tvergaard [21], Itskov and Aksel [17] and
Tsakmakis [37].
The aim of the present paper is to investigate the two aforementioned approaches to model
evolving anisotropy at finite strains. The basic kinematic description of anisotropic material by
Harrysson and Ristinmaa [13] and Harrysson et al. [14] serves as the point of departure here. In the
first approach the evolution of the directionally dependent properties is described in terms of
evolution laws for the structural variables. This approach is then compared with the one in which
additional evolution laws for the plastic velocity gradient are utilized. The approaches to modeling
evolving anisotropy that are described are investigated within a thermodynamic framework. A
general discussion of these two different approaches of describing the evolution of anisotropic
material is undertaken from a kinematic point of view. It is shown that the two approaches are
equivalent under certain specific conditions. To further compare these approaches, their behavior is
studied in two simple deformation processes: plain strain volume preserving deformation, and simple
shear. The aim here is to compare two different evolution laws found in the literature – one for the
substructure and one for the plastic velocity gradient – for situations in which no equivalence
between the approaches exists. For completeness the influence of the non-dissipative quantities for
the substructure will also be investigated. To obtain a general form for the non-dissipative quantities
the substructural force stemming from thermodynamical considerations needs to be analyzed.

3.1 Evolution laws describing directionally dependent properties
With the basis of thermodynamics at hand, the evolution laws describing the evolution of the
directionally dependent properties can be examined. Here two different approaches will be discussed
individually.

a. Plastic evolution of the continuum

as will be discussed in the following. However, starting of from a thermodynamic setting one can

The non-dissipative part for the continuum, i.e., Eq. (30) can be used in order to model the
evolution of anisotropy. This approach has been taken advantage of by a number of authors such as
Dafalias [7] and Kuroda [20]. The basic idea in using the plastic velocity gradient to model evolving
anisotropy is that, due to plastic deformation of the continuum, the material can rotate or spin around
the director vectors to describe the evolution of anisotropic material properties, cf., e.g. the
discussion of Dafalias [8], [9]. As evident from the above derivations, the spin term represents an
additional evolution law for the continuum. In this approach no evolution equation for the
substructure itself needs to be introduced.

3.2 Elastic isotropy
To further investigate the restrictions imposed by the second law of thermodynamics the restriction
of isotropic elasticity will be analyzed. If isotropic elasticity is considered no structural variables are
present. From Eq. (20.2) it can then be concluded that the structural force r(a) disappears, i.e., no
restrictions on the evolution laws for the plastic velocity gradient for the substructure are imposed,
cf. Eq. (22.2). This implies that from a thermodynamical standpoint kðaÞ can be chosen arbitrarily.
Furthermore, for the special case of isotropic elasticity it can be shown that the Mandel stress
tensor becomes symmetric, i.e., R ¼ RT ; cf. the discussion in [41]. Then, according to Eq. (31), the
non-dissipative part of lp becomes a skew-symmetric quantity. The approach of using elastic
isotropy in modeling evolving anisotropy has been used by, e.g., Kuroda [20], Han et al. [12] and
Sansour et al. [29].

4 Equivalence between the approaches
As discussed earlier we consider two different strategies to model the evolution of anisotropic
properties: one by stating evolution equations for the plastic velocity gradient, and the other by
forming evolution equations for the substructural velocity gradient. In this section the equivalence
between the approaches will be examined in detail.

5 Related models
In the previous sections the similarities of the two different approaches were investigated
analytically and for some very specific cases equivalence was found. The aim is now to further
compare different evolution laws, for the elastic velocity gradient and for the substructure, where
equivalence of the two cannot be shown, i.e., the situation (43). The material to be examined here
possesses cubic material symmetry which is a very simple form of anisotropic material symmetry.
The intention here is to investigate how the different forms of describing evolving anisotropy will
compare to each other and therefor the simple choice of material symmetry.

6 Conclusions
A comparison was made of two different approaches to the modeling of evolving anisotropic
material behavior. One approach took advantage of evolution laws for the substructural velocity
gradient, whereas the other utilized additional evolution laws for the plastic velocity gradient for the
continuum. In the development of evolution laws thermodynamic considerations were taken. The
similarities of the two approaches and the differences between them were discussed from a kinematic
point of view. Here, equivalence of the two approaches could be found for a specific situation.
To further analyze the two approaches, two non-equivalent evolution laws applied to simple
deformation processes were studied. For the particular deformation process examined it was found
that almost identical response was obtained from the two different approaches to model evolving

It can be concluded in general terms that the approach of using additional evolution laws for the
plastic velocity gradient of the continuum represents a special case of the approach of using
substructural plastic velocity gradients. In the case where the director vectors stay almost orthogonal
to each other the approach of using additional evolution laws for the continuum can be
recommended. This since it involves fewer evolution laws and therefore the numerical system to
solve is reduced.
In the general case, however, the approach involving use of evolution laws for the plastic velocity
gradients for the substructure is to be preferred since it provides a very general description of the
evolution of anisotropy. Here the non-orthogonality of the director vectors can be modeled in a
straightforward manner, since the linear maps describing the plastic evolution of the director vectors
can be general second order tensors and are not restricted to orthogonal tensors.
For completeness the non-dissipative quantity for the plastic velocity gradient for the substructure
was studied. The non-dissipative quantity for the substructure was found to generally be a nonsymmetric quantity. For the special material model employed the orthogonality of the director
vectors was found to be altered during the rolling process and simple shear. The proposed form of
the non-dissipative part for the substructure was compared with a form where the director vectors
were convected to the plastic deformation. This to compare the two forms as the latter one can not
easily be fitted into the framework presented here. It was shown that for the two models studied here,
the response was virtually identical.
