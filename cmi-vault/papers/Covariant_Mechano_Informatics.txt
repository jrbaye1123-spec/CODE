Materials science has a geometry problem that it does not yet know it has.
For half a century, the design of degradable biomaterials has proceeded by enumeration: synthesize a new composition, measure its degradation rate, fit a curve, publish.
This is not a theory—it is a catalog. Each entry in the catalog is a material with a name
(PLGA 85:15, PCL, gelatin methacrylate) and a parameter (a diffusivity D, a hydrolysis
rate k), but there is no principle that tells us why one material degrades faster than another,
or—more critically—why the same material degrades differently under different geometric

section it acts upon. When the section is the state of a degrading scaffold—its density ρ,

Specific Aim 4: Noether Charge Spectroscopy and Gauge Invariance Validation
We will map the GO G-band (∼1580 cm−1 ) polarization ratio I∥ /I⊥ as a function of radial
position at t = 0 and t = 28 days. The integral Qexp = ∑i ( I∥ /I⊥ )i ∆r must remain

gauge-theoretic mapping of GO to the spin connection ωba .
Significance. If validated, CMI establishes the first language for materials that know
themselves—a unified framework replacing empirical catalogs with geometric invariants.
The GO/PLGA-PEG/HA scaffold is the experimental apparatus; the measured α is the
answer. Failure at any of the five falsification criteria (Sections 6.4–6.6) is equally valuable:
it would demonstrate that self-reference in materials requires different categorical axioms
than those inherited from relational biology and gauge theory.
