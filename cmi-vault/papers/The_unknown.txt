Skyfall
The ramp opened at twenty thousand feet.
Sergeant Marcus Cole did not look down. He had learned, in twelve years of
airborne operations across four continents, that the ground was an abstraction until you hit it. What mattered was the interval between exiting the
aircraft and the chute deploying. Four seconds of controlled fall. Then the
jerk, the silence, the long drift down beneath a canopy of black nylon.
He had done this seventy-three times. He had never done it into an active
combat zone against an enemy whose physiology he did not understand.
The night sky over northern Scotland was unnaturally clear. Below them the
lights of the small coastal town of Wick flickered in the darkness—not the
steady glow of streetlamps and late-night windows, but something erratic.
Something that pulsed in a rhythm that was not quite random and not quite
patterned. Something that looked, from altitude, like a heartbeat.
Lieutenant Elena Reyes stood at the ramp’s edge, one gloved hand on the
release bar. She was twenty-six years old, three years out of West Point, and
Cole had read her file three times because he did not trust the brass to send
him someone useful. Top of her class in tactical planning. Distinguished
at the Infantry Basic Officer Course. Zero combat drops. The generals had
wanted an officer who could think under pressure. She could think. Whether
she could think while falling at terminal velocity toward an alien structure

“Two minutes,” Reyes said. Her voice was steady over the intercom. Cole

The twelve men in the cabin checked their gear with the mechanical repetition of soldiers who had done it a thousand times before. Rifles. Sidearms.
Comms gear. Medkits with tourniquets and clotting agents. The standardissue loadout for a night insertion into hostile territory.
But they were also carrying things none of them had ever carried into combat.
Portable spectrometers in hardened cases. Sealed biological sample containers cooled by liquid nitrogen. Radiation badges calibrated for spectra that the
physicists said might exist but had never been measured. The gear of scientists, not soldiers. The gear of people who did not know what they were
facing and were trying to prepare for everything.
Three hours earlier, a Royal Navy patrol boat—the HMS Severn, River-class,
hundred-man crew—had reported a light in the sky above the Pentland Firth.
Not an aircraft. Not a satellite. Not any known celestial phenomenon. The
light had descended, hovered for six minutes, and then settled on the coast
near Wick. The Severn had approached to investigate. Its last transmission
had been the words “visual contact, unknown structure” followed by thirty
seconds of audio that the analysts described as “structured noise.”
Then silence. The Severn had stopped reporting entirely.
Satellite overflight showed a structure on the coast where no structure had
existed twenty-four hours earlier. It was not a building in any conventional
sense. The thermal signature was wrong—too uniform, too cool for an active
installation, too warm for inert matter. The geometry did not resolve cleanly
in any imaging spectrum. The analysts had used words like “crystalline” and
“organic” and then, according to the intelligence officer who briefed Cole,
they had stopped using words altogether and simply sent the images up the
chain with a classification marking Cole had never seen before.
Cole had studied those images in the pre-flight briefing. He had also seen the
satellite photos of the Severn, drifting unmanned three miles offshore, its entire crew compartment fused into a single continuous surface. No seams. No
rivets. No entry hatches. Just a vessel that had become a solid block of what

Cole counted chutes in the moonlight. He counted them twice because the

interval between any two jumpers—and then nothing. Twelve men had exited
the aircraft. Twelve chutes should be visible. But there were only eleven.
The thirteenth soldier—Private First Class Thomas Nguyen, nineteen years

been cracking jokes about little green men during the pre-flight briefing,
the one who had asked Cole whether the aliens would respect the Geneva
Convention—his chute had not opened.
They watched him fall past them. A dark shape accelerating, limbs flailing
once and then stilling. He did not scream. The radio registered a single burst
of static at the moment of impact, and then the channel went quiet except for
the sound of eleven people breathing.
Cole landed in a field of heather two hundred meters from the structure’s
perimeter. He cut his chute with two slashes of his combat knife, unslung his
rifle, and began to move before his knees had fully absorbed the impact. The
heather was wet with sea mist. The ground was soft—good landing terrain,
the kind he would have chosen for a training drop.
The structure rose before him like something that had grown rather than
been built. Spires of material that shifted between deep blue and ultraviolet,
surfaces that seemed to ripple when observed directly but held still in peripheral vision. It was eighty meters across at its widest point, perhaps forty

Cole was the first to approach the structure’s surface. It was smooth, slightly

in his chest, in the cavity behind his sternum, in a place that felt interior in

perhaps, but responsive. Reactive. Aware.
Corporal Desta was running the spectrometer along the surface, the device
chattering to itself in clicks and whirs. “The material composition is shifting.
It’s not static. The surface is changing in response to proximity. It’s reading

“Reading us how?”
“I don’t know. But the signal pattern changed when you touched it. It’s—”
He stopped. “Sir, a section of the surface just dissolved.”
He was right. A portion of the structure’s exterior—roughly two meters wide
and three meters tall—had simply ceased to be material. Not retracted, not
opened, not moved aside. It had dissolved, the substance transitioning from
solid to nothing in the space of a heartbeat. Beyond it, a corridor of bluetinged darkness stretched inward, its walls pulsing with the same arrhythmia
as the structure’s exterior.
Cole went first. He had always gone first. It was not bravery; it was the
knowledge that if you asked other men to walk into the unknown, you had to
be willing to walk into it yourself. The air inside was warm—uncomfortably
warm, perhaps eighty-five degrees—and carried a smell that reminded him
of ozone after a thunderstorm. The walls were not walls in any architectural sense. They were surfaces that registered his presence, shifting color
as he passed from deep blue to pale violet, as though his passage left a wake
in the material itself. He had the distinct impression—immediate, visceral,
undeniable—that the structure was aware of him.

The Seven
The debriefing lasted six hours and answered nothing.
Reyes was taken to a separate room by men in suits who did not introduce
themselves. Desta’s spectrometer recordings were confiscated before he had
finished downloading them. Nguyen was evacuated to a medical facility that
Cole was not cleared to know about—somewhere in Germany, according to
the flight plan he glimpsed on the extraction pilot’s clipboard, but the clipboard was classified and he was not supposed to have seen it.
Cole himself spent four hours in a windowless room at RAF Lossiemouth,
repeating the same answers to the same questions from three different sets
of interrogators. What had he seen? What had he heard? What had the
creature looked like? What had Nguyen said? Why had he gone deeper into
the structure instead of securing the first chamber? Why had he not retrieved
one of the dead creatures? Why had he not called for extraction the moment
he realized Nguyen was alive?
He answered all of it. He was a soldier. Answering questions was part of the
job.
What he learned, in the spaces between the questions, from the things the
interrogators said to each other when they thought he was not listening, was
this: the Scotland structure was not unique. It was one of seven.
Simultaneous appearances. Coastal sites. Scotland, Brazil, Japan, South

The first real mission came ten days later. The Brazil structure—designated
Site Bravo, the second to appear—had begun emitting a new signal. Desta,
now formally in charge of the signals analysis section (which consisted of
himself, two analysts borrowed from the NSA, and a physicist from CERN
who had never worked with military hardware before), described it as “an
activation sequence, something’s about to happen.”
Cole led a squad of eight into the Amazon basin. The structure there was
larger than Scotland’s—a hundred and twenty meters across, its spires visible
above the rainforest canopy from five kilometers out. The Brazilian army
had established a cordon at ten kilometers and evacuated every settlement
within twenty. The jungle was empty except for the soldiers and the structure
and whatever was inside. The silence was deeper than any silence Cole had
experienced in combat—not the silence of concealment, but the silence of
absence. No birds. No insects. No animal calls. The rainforest, for kilometers
in every direction, had gone still.

structure’s interior in real time, projecting a three-dimensional model onto
the squad’s helmet displays. The engineering section had fabricated armor

whose name Cole had finally learned, two years into the war, and immediately forgotten because it did not matter; the General was the General, and

“The President wants to meet you. Wants to pin a medal on your chest. Wants
to make a speech about the heroes who saved the world.”
“No.”
“I told them you’d say that. I’ll handle the politicians. You handle the war.”
Cole nodded. The General turned to leave, then paused at the door.
“Colonel. What you did—what all of you did—there’s no precedent for it.
No doctrine. No manual. You built an organization that did the impossible
because no one told you it was impossible. Whatever comes next, we’ll face
it the same way.”
The General left. Cole remained at the display, watching the signals spread
across the galaxy like the first light of a new dawn. The war was not over.
But for the first time in two years, it felt like something other than a slow,
inevitable defeat. It felt like the beginning of something. Something larger
than Earth. Larger than humanity. Larger than any single species or world.
It felt like hope. Not the abstract hope of prayers and wishes, but the concrete
hope of people who had looked at an impossible problem and solved it anyway. The hope of soldiers who had volunteered for missions they knew they
would not survive. The hope of scientists who had worked through exhaustion and grief and failure until they found the answer. The hope of creatures
who had been enslaved for three centuries and had finally, impossibly, been
freed.
The hope of a species that had been tested and had not broken.
Cole turned away from the display. There was work to do. Missions to plan.
Soldiers to train. A galaxy to liberate. The memorial wall would need more
names before it was done. But the wall would endure. The organization
would endure. The species would endure.
And somewhere, in the darkness between the stars, on worlds that had never
heard of Earth or humanity or XCOM, enslaved beings were looking at the
