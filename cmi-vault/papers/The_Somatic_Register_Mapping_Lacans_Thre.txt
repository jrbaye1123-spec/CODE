The Somatic Register: Mapping Lacan’s Three Orders
onto Autonomic Physiology
June 2026
Position paper — correspondence welcome: rbaye1123@gmail.com
Abstract
Thayer and Lane’s neurovisceral integration model established that heart rate variability (HRV) indexes prefrontal-amygdala regulation of emotion. Interoception research
(Critchley, Garfinkel, Barrett) demonstrated that heartbeat detection accuracy predicts
emotional granularity. Polyvagal theory (Porges) connected vagal tone to social engagement. What remains absent is an interpretive framework that can hold the physiological data and the subjective structure of experience in a single model without reducing
one to the other. We propose that Lacan’s three registers—the Imaginary, Symbolic, and
Real—provide exactly this framework. Heart rate is the Real: a brute biological rhythm
that resists symbolization. Body image and interoceptive self-representation constitute
the Imaginary. Diagnostic categories, quantified-self metrics, and the language of psychophysiology form the Symbolic. The gap between these registers produces the phenomena we call anxiety, alexithymia, and depersonalization. We outline a research program
that treats resting heart rate as a fixed-point attractor for personality configuration, drawing on the phase-diagram methodology developed by Baye (2026) in the Moe complexity
framework.

1 The Empirical Foundation
Three independent research programs have converged on a single finding: the body’s autonomic state is not merely correlated with emotional experience—it is infrastructural to it.

1

1.1 The Neurovisceral Integration Model
Thayer and Lane (2000, 2009) proposed that HRV serves as an index of neurovisceral integration: the degree to which prefrontal inhibitory circuits can modulate subcortical threatdetection systems. High HRV indicates an intact vagal brake—the myelinated vagus can
rapidly down-regulate cardiac output in response to changing environmental demands. Low
HRV indicates a failure of this brake: the organism, once aroused, cannot return to baseline.
Meta-analyses confirm that low HRV is transdiagnostically associated with anxiety disorders, depression, PTSD, and borderline personality disorder (Koenig et al., 2016; Chalmers et
al., 2014). Critically, HRV is not merely a state marker of distress. It is stable within individuals over years (Bertsch et al., 2012; Li et al., 2019), with heritability estimates of approximately
0.47–0.50 (De Geus et al., 2019). It is a trait, not a symptom.

1.2 Interoception and Emotional Granularity
Interoception—the perception of internal body states—mediates the relationship between autonomic activity and felt emotion. Critchley et al. (2004) demonstrated that interoceptive accuracy (IA), measured via heartbeat detection tasks, correlates with insula activation and with
the intensity of emotional experience. Garfinkel et al. (2015) distinguished three dimensions:
interoceptive accuracy (objective detection), interoceptive sensibility (subjective confidence),
and interoceptive awareness (metacognitive calibration of the two).
Barrett (2017) integrated these findings into a predictive processing framework: emotions
are constructed from interoceptive predictions, not read out from a fixed set of innate circuits.
The brain continuously models the body’s expected state, and the match or mismatch between
prediction and interoceptive signal constitutes the raw material of emotional experience. In
this view, alexithymia—the inability to identify and describe emotions—is fundamentally an
interoceptive deficit: the body is sending signals that the Symbolic cannot name.

1.3 Low Resting Heart Rate and Fearless Dominance
Raine (1993, 2014) established that low resting heart rate (RHR) is the best-replicated biological
correlate of antisocial behavior and psychopathy. Prospective longitudinal studies (Portnoy &
Farrington, 2015) show that low RHR at age 3 predicts conduct disorder at age 11. The effect
size is substantial (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.56) and independent of confounds including BMI, physical
fitness, and socioeconomic status.
The leading interpretation is the low fear hypothesis: if threat does not register somatically—
if the heart does not race, the palms do not sweat—then threat does not register psychologi2

cally. Fear conditioning fails to take. Punishment does not deter. The superego, in psychoanalytic terms, never installs itself because the somatic marker for transgression is absent.

2 The Missing Framework: Why Lacan?
The empirical programs above are rich, precise, and mutually reinforcing. They share a limitation: they have no framework for the subject—for the entity that experiences the body’s
signals, that interprets them, that builds an identity around them or fails to.
Psychophysiology measures physiological variables. Cognitive neuroscience identifies
neural correlates. But neither addresses the structure of subjective experience: the gap between what the body is doing (Real), how one imagines oneself (Imaginary), and how one
names and categorizes experience (Symbolic). These are Lacan’s three registers, and they map
onto autonomic physiology with surprising precision.

2.1 The Real: Heart Rate as Brute Biology
The Real is that which resists symbolization absolutely. It is the kernel of experience that
cannot be put into words, cannot be integrated into the subject’s self-narrative, cannot be domesticated by interpretation. Heart rate is the Real par excellence: a rhythm generated by the
sinoatrial node, independent of conscious control, continuing during sleep, during anesthesia,
during the most elaborate symbolic acts. You cannot talk your heart rate down. You cannot
argue with it. It does not respond to interpretation.
When the Real of heart rate intrudes into awareness—palpitations, a racing pulse at rest,
the sensation that one’s heart is “too fast” or “too slow”—the Symbolic order is punctured.
The gap between “I am calm” (a Symbolic statement) and 90 BPM (a Real measurement) is the
structural locus of anxiety. In Lacanian terms, this is not the anxiety that signals a specific
danger but the anxiety that signals the proximity of the Real—what he called l’angoisse du réel.

2.2 The Imaginary: Body Image and Interoceptive Self-Representation
The Imaginary is the register of images, identifications, and the ego’s self-constitution through
the mirror stage. It is how the subject imagines themselves as a coherent whole. Applied to
the soma, the Imaginary includes:
• Body image: the visual-proprioceptive schema of one’s physical form.

3

• Interoceptive self-representation: the felt sense of one’s internal state. “I am an anxious
person” is an Imaginary identification with a recurring somatic pattern.
• The quantified-self transformation: when a Fitbit reports “resting heart rate: 72 BPM,”
the Imaginary appropriates this number. “My RHR is 72.” The Symbolic provides the
digit; the Imaginary makes it an identity.
Garfinkel’s distinction between interoceptive accuracy (Real: can you count your heartbeats?) and interoceptive sensibility (Imaginary: how confident are you that you can?) maps
cleanly onto the Real/Imaginary distinction. The metacognitive calibration between them—
awareness of the gap—is a higher-order Symbolic operation.

2.3 The Symbolic: Diagnostic Categories and the Naming of the Body
The Symbolic is the register of language, law, and the signifier. It is the system of differences through which experience is categorized. In the somatic domain, the Symbolic operates
through:
• Medical diagnosis: “bradycardia,” “tachycardia,” “sinus arrhythmia.”
• Psychiatric classification: “Generalized Anxiety Disorder,” “Panic Disorder,” “Somatic
Symptom Disorder.”
• Quantified-self discourse: “My HRV is 45 ms. That’s below average for my age.”
• Cultural norms: “A normal resting heart rate is 60–100 BPM.”
Verhaeghe (2002, 2012) argued that diagnostic categories do not merely describe conditions; they create subjectivities. When a patient receives a diagnosis of Panic Disorder, the
Symbolic has named the Real of their racing heart. This naming can be stabilizing—it provides
a framework, an explanation, a treatment protocol. But it can also be alienating: the diagnosis
becomes an identity. “I am a panic disorder patient” replaces “my heart races and I don’t know
why.”
The Symbolic never fully captures the Real. There is always a remainder—Lacan’s objet
a—that escapes signification. In the somatic domain, this remainder is the brute fact of the
heartbeat itself: the particular rhythm, the felt quality, the way it changes when you measure it. The quantified-self movement is the Symbolic’s most ambitious attempt to grasp this
remainder, and it reproduces the gap at a higher resolution.

3 The Phase-Diagram Approach
Baye (2026) developed a methodology in the Moe complexity framework: treat the system’s
configuration space as a phase diagram, identify fixed-point attractors, measure gradients, and
locate critical thresholds where qualitative transitions occur. The same methodology applies
to the soma-psyche interface.

3.1 Resting Heart Rate as a Fixed-Point Attractor
RHR is stable within individuals (test-retest r ≈ 0.7–0.8 over years). It acts as an attractor:
perturbations (exercise, stress, caffeine) shift heart rate temporarily, but the system returns
to its resting baseline. This baseline is not arbitrary. It represents a specific configuration of
the autonomic nervous system—a particular balance of sympathetic and parasympathetic tone
that defines the system’s default operating point.
Different RHR baselines correspond to different basins of attraction in the psychological
phase diagram. Low RHR (<60 BPM) creates a basin where threat signals are attenuated; the
organism seeks stimulation because baseline arousal is insufÏcient. Extremely low RHR (<50
BPM) creates a basin where fear conditioning fails entirely—the Raine effect. High RHR (>80
BPM) creates a basin where threat signals are amplified; the organism avoids stimulation because baseline arousal is already high.

3.2 The Critical Threshold Hypothesis
We hypothesize that there exists a critical RHR threshold—approximately 50–55 BPM—below
which the qualitative structure of personality reconfigures. Above this threshold, fear conditioning operates normally; punishment produces avoidance; the superego (in Freudian terms)
can install itself through internalized parental prohibitions. Below this threshold, the somatic
marker for threat is insufÏcient to drive avoidance learning. The result is not a quantitative
difference in fearfulness but a qualitative difference in the capacity for guilt.
This is directly analogous to the Λ-critical threshold in the Moe framework: a specific
value of the coupling constant (Λ) separates regimes where computational complexity classes
differ qualitatively (P vs. NP-hard). In both cases, a continuous biological parameter produces
a discontinuous psychological outcome.

5

3.3 HRV as an Order Parameter
If RHR is the fixed-point attractor, HRV is the order parameter: it measures the system’s capacity to leave the attractor. High HRV indicates a shallow basin—the system can respond
flexibly to perturbations, engage and disengage, accelerate and decelerate. Low HRV indicates a deep basin—the system is trapped near its attractor, and perturbations produce either
no response (freeze, dissociation) or an exaggerated one (panic, flooding) because the vagal
brake cannot modulate the transition.
This maps onto the clinical phenomenology: PTSD and depression are characterized by
low HRV and behavioral rigidity—the system cannot leave its attractor. Meditation and HRV
biofeedback training increase HRV and behavioral flexibility—they shallow the basin, enabling
transitions that were previously inaccessible.

4 Research Program
We propose four empirical investigations:

4.1 Study 1: RHR Distribution and Personality Structure
Recruit N = 500 healthy adults. Measure RHR (5-minute seated baseline), HRV (RMSSD, HFHRV), interoceptive accuracy (heartbeat tracking task), and personality (Big Five, Triarchic
Psychopathy Measure). Primary hypothesis: RHR will show a bimodal distribution with a
critical threshold at approximately 50–55 BPM, below which fearless dominance scores increase discontinuously. Secondary hypothesis: HRV will mediate the relationship between
RHR and neuroticism such that the combination of low RHR + high HRV predicts adaptive
sensation-seeking, while low RHR + low HRV predicts psychopathic traits.

4.2 Study 2: The Lacanian Registers as Measurable Constructs
Operationalize the three registers:
• Real: RHR, HRV, skin conductance response (SCR) — directly measured autonomic
variables.
• Imaginary: Interoceptive sensibility (confidence ratings on heartbeat task), body image questionnaires, self-report personality items that involve identification with somatic
states (“I am a calm person,” “I am an anxious person”).
6

• Symbolic: Diagnostic labels (PHQ-9, GAD-7), quantified-self metrics that the subject
endorses as self-descriptive, language use in free-response descriptions of bodily experience (analyzed via LIWC for category words, medical terminology, and emotional
labels).
Hypothesis: The gap between Real and Imaginary (calibrated as IA minus interoceptive
sensibility) predicts anxiety scores independent of absolute RHR. The gap between Imaginary
and Symbolic (discrepancy between felt experience and available vocabulary) predicts alexithymia scores.

4.3 Study 3: HRV Biofeedback and Register Realignment
Randomized controlled trial. Intervention: 8-week HRV biofeedback training with resonant
frequency breathing. Control: sham biofeedback (random visual feedback). Primary outcome: change in the IA-to-interoceptive-sensibility gap. Secondary outcomes: anxiety (GAD7), alexithymia (TAS-20), emotional granularity (experience sampling of emotion labels). Hypothesis: biofeedback reduces the Real-Imaginary gap, and this reduction mediates improvements in anxiety and emotional granularity.

4.4 Study 4: Longitudinal RHR and Identity Formation
Adolescent cohort (N = 200, ages 12–18), followed for 5 years with annual RHR, HRV, and
personality assessments. Hypothesis: individuals whose RHR crosses the critical threshold
(either upward or downward) during adolescence will show corresponding shifts in personality structure, specifically in traits related to fear, inhibition, and sensation-seeking. Those
whose RHR remains stable will show corresponding personality stability.

5 Conclusion
The neurovisceral integration model provides the physiological foundation. Interoception research provides the mechanism. What is missing is a framework that can hold the body’s Real,
the self’s Imaginary, and the culture’s Symbolic in a single coherent model. Lacan’s three registers, operationalized through measurable constructs, provide exactly this framework.
The resting heart rate is not merely a number. It is a fixed-point attractor for personality
configuration. It is the Real that the Symbolic names but never captures. It is the somatic
ground from which the subject emerges—and against which the subject defines itself.
