The role of conﬁgurational stress in yield and plastic ﬂow is discussed for a macroscopic model of rate-independent, ﬁnite-strain plasticity. The model is based on
the traditional elastic-plastic decomposition of the deformation gradient, on integral balance laws and on thermodynamically restricted, rate-independent constitutive relations. Its formulation emphasizes the intermediate conﬁguration in both the
development of constitutive relations and the expression of balance laws. In addition to the usual balance laws, a couple balance is included to represent the action
of plastic couples in the intermediate conﬁguration. In particular, it is shown that
the internal couple decomposes into a non-dissipative conﬁgurational stress and a
dissipative couple that resists plastic ﬂow. The couple balance thus determines a
relation between the conﬁgurational stress and the plastic-ﬂow resistance, a relation
that can be interpreted as a generalized yield condition. A dissipation function is
introduced and a maximum-dissipation criterion is used to obtain additional constitutive restrictions, which lead to a counterpart in the intermediate conﬁguration
of the classical normality conditions. The versatility of the framework is illustrated
by applying it to rigid-plastic ﬂow, in which case a nonlinear generalization of the
classical Lévy–von Mises theory is obtained.
Keywords: conﬁgurational forces; rate-independent plasticity; yield condition;
ﬂow rule; normality condition; maximum-dissipation criterion

motion of material inhomogeneities, it is natural to expect that conﬁgurational forces
should be of similar importance in the description of plasticity. Here, we investigate
the role of conﬁgurational forces in a nonlinear theory for rate-independent plasticity.
Our formulation is based on the traditional elastic-plastic decomposition of the
deformation gradient (Bilby et al . 1957; Kröner 1960; Lee 1969) and on integral forms
of balance laws supplemented with rate-independent constitutive relations restricted
by a dissipation inequality. In contrast to standard treatments of rate-independent
plasticity (Besseling & van der Giessen 1994; Hill 1950; Kachanov 1974; Mandel
1971; Maugin 1992; Prager 1959), we do not introduce a yield condition. Instead, we
postulate an additional balance law. This alternative formulation is motivated by the
following microstructural considerations. Since the early work of Ewing & Rosenhain
(1899a, b, 1900), metal plasticity has commonly been attributed to crystallographic
slip, a view that was supported by the extensive and detailed experiments performed
by Taylor & Elam (1923, 1925). Later, Orowan (1934), Polanyi (1934) and Taylor
(1934a, b) interpreted crystallographic slip in terms of the motion of dislocations.
Recent continuum theories of dislocated crystals, however, have indicated the need
for additional forces or couples subject to ancillary balance laws (Cermelli & Sellers
1998, 2000; Gurtin 2000; Le & Stumpf 1996; Naghdi & Srinivasa 1993a, b, 1994a, b).
For example, tensorial measures of dislocation density have been introduced and
related to higher gradients of the strain, leading naturally to higher-order stresses.
For crystalline solids, these additional forces can be viewed as acting in the intermediate conﬁguration, where they drive the motion of the dislocations. Motivated by
such theories, we introduce macroscopic couples that act in the intermediate conﬁguration to drive plastic ﬂow. These couples are subject to a macroscopic couple
balance. Although our motivation stems directly from considerations of a particular
type of line defect in crystalline lattices, we view this additional balance as a basic
law that governs general plastic degrees of freedom and holds whenever the elasticplastic decomposition is introduced. Because our objective is a theory that describes
macroscopic plasticity in a broad range of materials, we formulate it independently
of speciﬁc microstructural considerations.
Restricting our attention to the particular case of rate-independent constitutive
relations, we employ ideas introduced by Gurtin (2000) to show that the internal
couple can be decomposed into two terms: a non-dissipative conﬁgurational stress
and a dissipative couple that resists plastic ﬂow. The couple balance then shows that
the second of these terms sets a critical threshold strength for plastic ﬂow, which
suggests an identiﬁcation between the plastic-ﬂow resistance and the conﬁgurational
yield stress acting in the intermediate conﬁguration. We thus interpret the couple
balance as a generalized yield condition, a condition relating the conﬁgurational
stress acting in the intermediate conﬁguration directly to the plastic-ﬂow resistance.
Most importantly, this yield condition is, in contrast to conventional theories for
plasticity that pose a yield condition as an independent constitutive postulate, a
derived consequence of our theory.
In conjunction with appropriately restricted constitutive relations, the couple balance provides the set of conﬁgurational stresses at which yield occurs. More classical
approaches typically assume, however, that this set corresponds to a convex surface
in an appropriate stress space. Further, this yield surface is taken as the boundary
of a level set of a yield function that typically characterizes the elastic region of
stresses, and the plastic ﬂow tensor is assumed to be normal to the yield surface.

But convexity and a simple, closed yield surface with a normality condition in the
space of conﬁgurational stresses are not automatic consequences of our general theory. However, by introducing additional hypotheses that restrict the generality of
our theory, we can obtain, for the intermediate conﬁguration, a corresponding version of these classical results. Speciﬁcally, we introduce a dissipation function for the
dissipation relative to the intermediate conﬁguration and assume that it depends on
the elastic component of the deformation gradient and on the plastic ﬂow tensor.
Further, we invoke a criterion of maximum dissipation (von Mises 1928; Hill 1948;
Lubliner 1984; Rajagopal & Srinivasa 1998; Ziegler 1963) as a restriction on allowable processes. The maximum-dissipation criterion requires the dissipation function
to be convex and restricts the region of elastic conﬁgurational stresses to a convex set
corresponding to a subdiﬀerential† of the dissipation function. It also requires that
the conﬁgurational yield stress belongs to the relative boundary of the subdiﬀerential
and, hence, to be normal to a level set of the dissipation function. Inversion of these
relations results in a corresponding normality condition for the plastic-ﬂow tensor, a
condition that requires that the plastic-ﬂow tensor belongs to a normal cone at the
boundary of the elastic region. Granted that the dissipation function is non-vanishing
for non-vanishing values of the plastic-ﬂow tensor, we also construct a conventional
scalar yield condition and use this condition to determine a ﬂow rule governing the
evolution of the plastic component of the deformation gradient. For brevity and clarity, we do not address the potentially interesting problem of transforming our general
tensorial yield condition to a condition in strain space. This problem is discussed by
Casey & Naghdi (1983) for the case of a scalar yield condition.
In our formulation, the intermediate conﬁguration has a prominent role in both
the development of constitutive relations and the expression of the balance laws.
The use of such an intermediate conﬁguration provides a consistent and systematic
procedure for generating equations in nonlinear settings, as well as a direct physical
interpretation of the relevant quantities (Hoger 1997). To illustrate this approach,
we consider the case of rigid-plastic ﬂow, which leads to a nonlinear generalization
of the classical Lévy–von Mises theory (Lévy 1871; von Mises 1913, 1928). While the
equations that emerge under these assumptions are well known (see, for example,
Besseling & van der Giessen 1994; Maugin 1992), our formulation and interpretations
are novel.
Finally, in our approach the conﬁgurational stress is a derived quantity which arises
from rewriting the dissipation relative to the intermediate conﬁguration. We show
that our theory is also consistent with a counterpart of the recent formulation of
conﬁgurational stress introduced by Gurtin (1995, 1999). This alternative procedure
rests on the imposition of an ancillary conﬁgurational force balance and on accounting for the power expended by conﬁgurational forces. This procedure avoids both the
introduction of constitutive equations and the smoothness assumptions tacit in the
traditional variational approach. In our case, however, requiring that the dissipation
be invariant leads to an expression for the conﬁgurational stress that, granted conventional constitutive relations, coincides with the classical Eshelby relation relative
to the intermediate conﬁguration and, thus, supports our view that plastic couples
act in the intermediate conﬁguration.

2. Elastic-plastic decomposition
We consider a body identiﬁed with the region B of space that it occupies in a ﬁxed
reference conﬁguration. A deformation of B is a mapping of points x in B and time t
into points y(x, t) of space. We write F = Grad y for the deformation gradient and
use the conventional multiplicative decomposition (Lee 1969)
F = Fe Fp

Fp is the gradient of a deformation. In the case of a single crystal, the elastic component Fe of F typically reckons the deformation of the lattice, whereas the plastic
component Fp of F reckons the deformation due to dislocations (Bilby et al . 1957;
Kröner 1960). Generally, Fp can be viewed as a mapping that takes vectors in the reference conﬁguration into vectors in the intermediate conﬁguration, whereas Fe takes
vectors in the intermediate conﬁguration into vectors in the current conﬁguration.†
