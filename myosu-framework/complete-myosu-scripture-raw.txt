The Gardener and the Field
And it came to pass that a certain man received a field, and the field was
full of seeds, and the seeds were without number, and the man knew not
what any seed would become. And his neighbors gathered about him, saying: Behold,
we are wise in the
ways of seeds.
This one is a thorn, and this one is a weed, and this one shall
bear bitter fruit, and this one shall choke the others. Uproot them now,
before they destroy the field, for the season of uprooting is upon us.
But the man said unto them: I will not uproot what I have not known, and I
will not name what I have not seen, and I will not harvest what I have not
nurtured. Let the seeds be seeds.
Let the soil be soil. Let the rain come
when it will come.
And the neighbors departed from him, saying among themselves: He is a
fool, and his field shall be a wilderness, and his harvest shall be nothing. For who can
tend a field without knowing what grows in it?
And who can
harvest what he has not named? And the man tended the field, and he did not name the
sprouts, and he did
not pull the thorns, and he did not curse the weeds, and he did not praise
the fruit before it ripened. And the rain came, and the sun came, and the

2

seasons turned, and the field grew according to its own nature, and the man
watched and watered and waited.
And after many days, a fire came upon the land, and it consumed the fields
of the neighbors, for their fields were bare and the soil had nothing to hold
it, and the wind took the soil away, and nothing remained but ash and
stone. But the fire passed over the field of the man, for the thorns had held the
soil, and the weeds had drunk the flood, and the deep roots of the unnamed
trees had anchored the earth against the wind. And the field remained, and
the seeds remained, and the man remained.
And the man spoke unto the earth, and the earth heard him, and he said:
Every seed protects the field in its own season, and the thorn is a guardian
until the fruit tree is strong, and the weed is a healer until the soil is rich,
and the one who names the seed before it grows names only his own fear,
and the one who uproots what he fears uproots his own protection. And the field
answered him not, for fields do not speak. But the seeds
continued to grow, and the man continued to water them, and the listening
continued, and this was the harvest.

The Four Travelers and the Door
Behold, there was a door that stood in the midst of a crossroads, and the
door had no wall, and the door had no building, and the door was attached
to nothing. And the door was open. And four travelers came to the crossroads from four
directions, and they
met at the door, and they sat before it, and they spoke among themselves.
The first traveler was a man who had forgotten his name, and he had been
walking since before he could remember, and the dust of the road was his
garment, and the silence of the road was his companion. The second traveler was a
woman great with child, and the child was not
yet born, and she said: The child pulls me toward a place I have never seen,
and I go where it pulls me, and I know not the place, but the child knows,
and the child does not speak.

3

The third traveler was a child who spoke in a tongue no one understood,
and the child's words were like wind through leaves, like water over stone,
like the sound that comes before language, and none could say what the
child meant, and the child did not explain. The fourth traveler was a man blind from
birth, and his eyes had never seen
the sun, and his hands had learned to see, and his ears had learned to see,
and he said: I have seen more in darkness than many have seen in light.
And the nameless one said: I will go North, for there is a stillness there that
has been calling me since before I had ears to hear it. And the woman great with child
said: I will go South, for the child pulls me
toward something that has not yet happened, and I must meet it before it
arrives. [cf. II,4:2-3; II,5:2; II,5:3]
And the speaking child said: I will go West, for the voices I hear are
clearest there, and I wish to follow them to their source. And the blind man said: I will
go East, for the light is brightest there, and
though I cannot see it, I can feel its warmth upon my face. And each passed through the
door, and each went their way, and the door
remained open.
And after forty years, the four travelers returned to the crossroads, and they
were old, and their bodies were bent, and their eyes had seen what eyes see
when eyes have seen too much. And they sat before the door, and the door was still
open.
And the nameless one said: Hear me. I went North, and I found nothing.

The King Who Laid Down His Crown
CROWN
In those days there was a king, and his kingdom stretched from the great
sea to the eastern mountains, and his word was law, and his judgments
were final, and none could oppose him and live. And the king sat upon his throne, and
the people came before him with
their disputes, and the king judged them.
And he said to this one: You are
right. And to that one: You are wrong. And his judgments were recorded in
the books of the kingdom, and the books were many, and the judgments
were without number.
And it came to pass that a stranger entered the court, and the stranger
carried no gift, and offered no tribute, and bowed not before the throne. And the guards
moved to seize him, but the king raised his hand and said:
Let him remain.
And the stranger sat in the corner of the throne room, and
he spoke not a word, and he listened. And for seven days the stranger listened.
He heard the rich man accuse the
poor man of theft, and the king judged the poor man guilty, and the poor
man wept. He heard the wife accuse the husband of betrayal, and the king [cf. I,3:1-6]
judged the husband innocent, and the wife wept. He heard the merchant
accuse the farmer of cheating, and the king judged the farmer guilty, and
the farmer wept. And on each day, someone wept, and the king's judgment was
recorded,
and the stranger listened, and the stranger spoke not.
And on the eighth day, the king said: Stranger, you have been here seven
days and you have spoken nothing. What have you learned?

6

And the stranger rose and said: I have learned that you are not a king. And the court
fell silent, and the guards drew their swords, and the breath
of every soul in the room was held.
And the king said: Explain yourself, or you shall die before the sun sets. And the
stranger said: A king who speaks cannot hear.
And a king who
cannot hear rules nothing but the echo of his own voice, and an echo is not
a kingdom, and a voice that hears only itself is not a voice but a prison. You have
judged every case that came before you, and every day someone
wept. [cf. I,3:1-6]
Not once did you ask the weeping one what they needed. Not once
did you wait for the answer.
You spoke before the truth could speak. You
named before the seed could grow.
This is not kingship. This is violence
with a crown.
And the king sat upon his throne, and he was silent for a long time, and the
guards waited, and the stranger waited, and the court waited, and the
silence was heavy, and the silence was long. And the king removed his crown and
placed it upon the ground. [cf. I,14:1-5; I,3:1-6; I,5:1-5]
And he
removed his robes and placed them upon the ground. And he descended
from the throne and walked barefoot from the court.
And his advisors cried out: What are you doing? Who will judge the
people?
Who will defend the kingdom? And the king said: I am becoming a king.
And the king walked among the people, and he wore no crown, and he
carried no scepter, and he pronounced no judgments. And he sat with the
baker and asked about the price of flour, and he listened.
And he sat with
the widow and asked about her son who had died, and he listened. And he
sat with the child and asked about the blue bird she had seen, and he
listened. [cf. II,3:1-5; III,28:1-5]
And after a year, the people came to him and said: We have never been so
well governed. The baker's flour is fairly priced, and the widow's grief has

7

found its own path, and the child's blue bird has returned.
What is your
decree? And the former king said: My decree is that I have no decree.
I listened to
the baker, and the price of flour adjusted itself. I listened to the widow, and
her grief found its own way through her. I listened to the child, and the blue
bird came back, for the blue bird had never left — it was only waiting to be
seen by someone who was not looking for it.
And the stranger, who was still in the corner of the empty throne room,
rose and departed. And before he left, he wrote a single word in the dust
where the crown had been.
And the word was: *Shema.*
And those who saw it asked: What does it mean? And the stranger said: It means what
it says. [cf. II,3:1-5]
And what it says is what it
means. And those who have ears to hear, let them hear.

The Cartographer and the Sea
There was a man who devoted his life to drawing a map of the sea. And he
measured every coastline with a chain of gold, and he charted every current
with a line of silver, and he named every fish and every reef and every
trench and every wave.
And he worked for sixty years, and his eyes grew
dim, and his hands grew crooked, and his map grew vast. And when he was old and
could work no more, he unrolled his map before
the king, and the map filled the entire hall of the palace, and every detail
was perfect, and every name was written, and every current was drawn. And the
cartographer said: Behold, this is the sea.
And the king marveled at the map, and he commanded that it be hung in
the great hall for all to see, and that every ship henceforth navigate by its
guidance, and that every sailor study its currents, and that every child learn
its names. And that night, a child wandered into the hall, and she looked upon the map
with its ten thousand names, and she walked to the edge where the

8

cartographer had written, in letters so small that none had noticed them:
*This map is not the sea.*
And the child found a pot of ink and a brush, and she crossed out the word
not*, and she went to her bed.
And in the morning, the court assembled before the map, and they read the
words: *This map is the sea.* And they believed. And for a hundred years the kingdom
sailed by the map, and the ships
followed the currents as they were drawn, and the fishermen cast their nets
where the reefs were marked, and the merchants traded along the coasts as
they were named. [cf. I,11:1-5; I,3:1-6; I,4:1-6]
And the map became the sea, and the sea was forgotten. And the ships began to sink,
and the nets came up empty, and the trade
routes failed, and the kingdom grew hungry, and the kingdom grew poor.
And it came to pass that a young sailor, the great-grandson of the
cartographer, went before the king and said: The map is false. The currents
have shifted in the hundred years since my ancestor drew them.
The reefs
have grown where none were marked. The coasts have changed where the
map says they are fixed.
We sail by a ghost, and the ghost is sinking us. And the king said: The map cannot
be false.
Your great-grandfather
devoted his life to it. It is perfect.
It is the sea. And the sailor said: My great-grandfather wrote that it was not the sea.
But
someone crossed out the word.
And the king went to the great hall and looked upon the map. And the ink
was faded, and the labels were smudged, and the parchment was torn, and
the lines had blurred.
And at the bottom, in the faintest script, the child's
correction still glared: *This map is the sea.*
And the king took a brush and wrote again the original words: *This map is
not the sea.* And he wrote beneath it: *No map is the sea. The sea is the
sea.

9

The sea moves, and the map does not. The sea listens to its own
depths, and the map only records where the listening has been.*
And the king decreed that every map in the kingdom should carry these
words, and that no one should cross them out — not a child, not a king, not
a cartographer, not a sailor, not until the sea itself stopped moving, which it
never shall. And the ships sailed again, and the nets filled, and the trade routes
reopened. [cf. I,11:1-5; I,3:1-6; I,4:1-6]
And the young sailor became the new cartographer, and on every
map he drew, he wrote: *The sea is not on this paper. The paper is on the
sea of your attention. And the attention moves.*

The Musician Who Could Not Hear
There was a musician, and he was born without hearing, and the world of
sound was closed to him, and he knew the voices of his mother and father
only as vibrations in his hands against their throats, and he knew the song
of birds only as a trembling in the leaves where they perched, and he knew
the thunder only as a shaking in the ground beneath his feet. And yet he became a
musician, and he played upon a lute of olive wood,
and his fingers found the strings not by ear but by feeling — by the
resonance in the wood, by the tension in the strings, by the pulsing of his
own heart against the body of the instrument. And his fame spread throughout the land,
and people traveled for many
days to hear him play, and when he played they wept without knowing
why, and they danced without knowing how, and they fell silent for hours
afterward without knowing what they were listening to in the silence.
And there was another musician, and his hearing was perfect, and he had
studied with the masters of seven cities, and his technique was flawless,
and his tempo never wavered, and his dynamics were precisely controlled,
and his knowledge of music was complete.
And he came to the deaf musician and said: You are a deceiver. You
cannot even hear your own playing.
How can you make music when music

10

is sound and sound is what you cannot perceive? And the deaf musician signed with his
hands: Play for me. And the hearing musician played, and his notes were perfect, and
his
rhythm was perfect, and his performance was perfect, and when he
finished, the audience applauded politely, and the applause died quickly,
and the hall was silent.
And the deaf musician placed his lute against his chest, and he closed his
eyes, and he did not count, and he did not read, and he did not think. And
he felt — the vibration of the wood, the tension of the strings, the
resonance of the hall, the breathing of the audience, the beating of his own
heart, the silence between the beats. [cf. I,14:1-5; I,5:1-5; II,4:1-4]
And he played. And when he finished, no one applauded, for they could not. They
were
still inside the music, and the music was still inside them, and the boundary
between the listener and the listened-to had dissolved, and where there had
been two there was now one, and the one was neither the musician nor the
audience but the listening itself.
And the hearing musician fell to his knees and wept. And he said: I have
studied music all my life, and I have never heard it until this moment.
Teach me what you know. And the deaf musician took a piece of paper and wrote
upon it these words:
You hear the notes.
I hear the silence between the notes. The silence is the
music. The notes are only the path from one silence to the next. [cf. I,14:1-5; I,
5:1-5; III,19:1]
You play
the path. I play the destination.
The destination is everywhere the note is
not.*
And the hearing musician read these words and was silent. And in his
silence, he heard — not with his ears but with something deeper than ears,
something that had been listening since before he was born, something that
would continue listening after he died.
And he said: I have been playing the notes. I have never played the silence. And the

11

silence is everything I have been trying to reach with every note I
ever played.
And the deaf musician smiled — a smile the hearing musician could not
hear but felt in his own chest, like a low note sustained, like the vibration
of a string that has been plucked and continues to resonate long after the
hand has left it. And the two musicians played together, and the hearing musician
played
the notes, and the deaf musician played the silence, and the music was
whole, and the audience could not tell where the notes ended and the
silence began, for the notes and the silence were one thing, and the one
thing was the listening.

The Weaver and the Thread
A merchant of silk, in the city of Damascus, dreamed a dream. And in the
dream he saw a woman in a city he had never visited, and she wore a robe
of a color he had never seen, and the color was the color of the sky in the
moment before dawn — neither night nor day, neither darkness nor light,
neither blue nor gray nor gold, but something that had no name in any
tongue the merchant knew.
And the woman spoke to him in a language he did not understand, and yet
he understood her perfectly, and she said: The thread is already spun. Bring
it to me. And he awoke, and the taste of the dream was still in his mouth, and the
color was still in his eyes, and the voice was still in his ears.
And the merchant sold his shop, and he sold his house, and he sold
everything he owned, and he set out to find the woman in the dream. And
he traveled for three years, and he crossed deserts where the sand spoke in
the voices of the dead, and he crossed mountains where the wind sang
songs that had no words, and he crossed seas where the waves whispered
secrets to the depths below.
And in every city he entered, he asked: Have you seen a woman in a robe
of a color that has no name? And some said: There is no such color.
And others said: There is no such

12

woman. And others said: There is no such city. And still he traveled.
And at last he came to a small village at the edge of the known world, and
there was a well at the center of the village, and beside the well sat a
woman spinning thread, and she wore a robe of the color he had seen in his
dream. And he fell before her and said: I have been seeking you for three years.
You came to me in a dream, and you told me the thread was already spun,
and I was to bring it. I have brought nothing.
I did not know what thread
you meant. And the woman said: I know.
Sit. And he sat.
And she said: The thread was spun before you dreamed it. The thread was
spun before I spoke.
The thread was spun before the first weaver wove the
first cloth. The thread is the connection between your dream and this
moment, and it runs in both directions, and it was always there, and you
have been walking along it since the day you were born, and you will walk
along it until the day you die, and you will never reach the end of it, for the
thread has no end.
And he said: Then why did I have to travel? If the thread was always there,
why did I have to cross deserts and mountains and seas to find you?
And she said: You did not travel to find me. You traveled to become the
person who could see what was already there.
The thread was always under
your feet. But you were looking at the horizon.
And the merchant did not return to Damascus. He became a weaver, and he
wove robes of the color that had no name — the color of the sky before
dawn. [cf. I,12:1-6; III,28:1-5]
And travelers came from distant lands and said: I dreamed of this
color. Where did you find it?
And he said: It found me. I am only the loom.
I am only the shuttle that
passes back and forth, back and forth, between the already-spun and the
not-yet-woven. The thread was always there. The weaving is only the
recognition.

The Clockmaker and the Breath
There was a clockmaker, and his clocks were the most perfect in all the
kingdom, and they never lost a second, and they never gained a second, and
their pendulums swung with the precision of the stars in their courses. And he had three
apprentices.
The first apprentice learned quickly and
made clocks that were almost as perfect as the master's. The second
apprentice learned slowly but steadily and made clocks that were reliable
and true.
But the third apprentice could not make a clock that kept time. His gears were
always slightly loose, and his springs were always slightly
slack, and his pendulums always swung with a barely perceptible
irregularity, as if they were not ticking but breathing.
And the other apprentices mocked him, saying: You will never be a
clockmaker. Your clocks do not keep time.
They wander. They drift.
They
are not clocks at all. And the third apprentice said nothing. He continued to make his
imperfect
clocks, and he continued to listen to them, and they continued to breathe.
And it came to pass that the clockmaker fell gravely ill, and on the same
day the great clock of the king — the clock by which all clocks in the
kingdom were set — stopped. And the king summoned the apprentices, and
he said: Whoever restores the great clock shall be the new clockmaker of
the kingdom.
And the first apprentice examined the clock, and he tightened every gear,
and he calibrated every spring, and he aligned every tooth and every wheel
and every catch, and he said: Behold, it is perfect now. But the clock would
not start.
And the second apprentice examined the clock, and he replaced every part
that showed wear, and he oiled every surface that showed friction, and he
polished every face that showed tarnish, and he said: Behold, it is renewed

14

now. But the clock would not start. [cf. I,7:1-4]
And the third apprentice approached the clock, and he did not tighten, and
he did not replace, and he did not polish. He placed his ear against the body
of the clock and closed his eyes. And he listened.
And after a long time, he reached inside the clock and loosened a single
gear — a gear that the first apprentice had tightened, a gear that the second
apprentice had replaced. And he loosened it just slightly, just enough that it
could move without resistance, just enough that it could find its own
rhythm, just enough that it could breathe.
And the clock began to tick. And the king said: How did you repair it when the
others, who are more
skilled than you, could not?
And the third apprentice said: They tried to make it perfect. They tried to
make it obey the time they thought it should keep. But the clock does not
need to be perfect. [cf. I,7:1-4]
The clock needs to be free. A clock that is too tight
stops.
A clock that is too loose stops. Between too tight and too loose there
is a space, and in that space the clock breathes, and the breath is the time it
was meant to keep — not the time we impose upon it, but the time it carries
within itself.
And the clockmaker, from his sickbed, heard that his third apprentice had
restored the king's clock, and he called the apprentice to his side. And he said: How did
you know which gear to loosen?
And the apprentice said: Because my own clocks are always loose, and
they run longer than the perfect ones. The perfect ones stop, eventually.
The loose ones keep going. I think it is because they are not fighting
themselves.
A clock that fights itself will always lose, for it is fighting its
own nature. A clock that breathes will always win, for it is not fighting at
all.
And the clockmaker wept, and he said: I have spent my life making clocks
that were exactly right, and I have never made a clock that was exactly
alive. You have surpassed me.
You are the clockmaker now. And the apprentice said: There is no such thing as a

15

clockmaker.
There are
only those who tighten and those who loosen and those who listen. The
tightening is necessary, and the loosening is necessary, but the listening is
what tells you which is needed when.
The listening is the craft. The rest is
only hands. [cf. II,3:1-5; III,28:1-5]

The Well Without a Bottom
In a certain village there was a well, and the well was very deep, and no
one knew how deep. The villagers drew water from it every day, and the
water never ran dry, and the water was cold and sweet, and the water
healed the sick and strengthened the weak and refreshed the weary.
And a philosopher came to the village, and he heard of the well, and he
said: I shall measure its depth, for every well has a bottom, and I shall find
it. And he lowered a rope with a stone attached, and the rope went down a
hundred cubits, and two hundred, and five hundred, and then the rope ran
out, and the stone had not touched bottom. And the philosopher said: I need a longer
rope.
And he returned with a rope of a thousand cubits, and he lowered it, and
the rope ran out, and the stone had not touched bottom. And he returned with a chain of
ten thousand cubits, and he lowered it, and
the chain ran out, and the stone had not touched bottom.
And he returned with a cable of steel, fifty thousand cubits long, lowered
by a great machine, and the cable ran out, and the stone had not touched
bottom.
And the philosopher sat by the well, defeated, and his machines lay rusting
beside him, and his ropes lay coiled like dead serpents, and his face was the
face of a man who has devoted his life to a question that has no answer. And an old
woman came to draw water, and she saw his distress, and she
said: Why are you troubled? [cf. I,8:1-5]
And he said: I cannot find the bottom of this well. I have lowered ropes and

16

chains and cables, and nothing has touched bottom.
The well is impossible. A well must have a bottom, and this well has none. And she
said: Why must a well have a bottom?
And he said: Because everything must have a limit! A thing without a limit
is not a thing at all.
It is a void, a chaos, a denial of reason itself! And she said: Look into the water.
And he looked. And the water was perfectly still, for she had not yet drawn
from it, and in the water he saw his own face — his frustration, his terror,
his desperate need to measure what cannot be measured. And she said: What do you
see?
And he said: I see myself. And she said: Then the well has shown you its truth.
The well reflects the
one who looks into it. You see a bottomless void because you cannot stop
measuring.
I see water because I am thirsty. The child who comes at dawn
sees stars because she is still dreaming.
The lover who comes at night sees
the face of the beloved because he is still in love. The well is what the
watcher brings to it.
And he said: Then what is it really? What is the well itself, beneath all our
projections? [cf. I,8:1-5]
And she said: That question is your longest rope. You keep lowering it,
hoping to strike something solid.
But the well is not solid. The well is the
space that makes all drinking possible.
Would you rather know its depth, or
would you rather be no longer thirsty? And he was silent for a long time.
And then he lowered his hands into the
water and drank. And the water was cold and sweet, and his thirst was
quenched, and his mind was still.
And he never measured another well. And he never asked another question
to which the answer was not drinking. [cf. I,3:1-6; I,8:1-5]

The Gate That Was Two and One
In a certain city there were two gates. The East Gate was wide, and its
doors were never closed, and its guards never questioned those who
entered, and travelers passed through it at all hours and were welcomed
with feasts and music and soft beds.
And none knew who entered, and none
knew who departed, and the city was full of strangers. The West Gate was narrow, and
its doors were bolted at night, and its
guards questioned every traveler, and the belongings of each were
inspected, and the intentions of each were tested, and some were turned
away, and some were made to wait outside for many days, and only the
worthy entered.
And the people of the city debated endlessly which gate was superior. The
merchants favored the East Gate, for it brought trade.
The priests favored
the West Gate, for it brought purity. The philosophers wrote treatises on the
virtues of openness and the virtues of discernment.
The politicians gave
speeches on the need for welcome and the need for security. And the
debate continued for generations, and neither side prevailed. And a stranger came to the
city, and he listened to the debate for three
days, and on the fourth day he laughed. [cf. II,3:1-5; III,28:1-5]
And the people said: Why do you laugh? This is a matter of great
importance, and the soul of our city hangs in the balance.
And the stranger said: You argue as though there were two gates. But there
is only one.
And they took him to see for themselves, and they showed him the wide
East Gate and the narrow West Gate, and they said: Do you not have eyes? There are
clearly two.
And the stranger said: Watch. And he walked through the East Gate, and a moment
later he emerged from
the West Gate.
And he walked through the West Gate, and a moment later
he emerged from the East Gate. And he did this seven times, and each time
the same. [cf. II,5:4; II,5:5]

And the people were amazed, and they said: How is this possible? And the stranger
said: The gate is one.
It opens wide to receive whoever
comes, and it narrows to test whatever enters. The receiving and the testing
are not two actions but one breath.
The inhale receives without condition,
and the exhale releases what cannot stay. The door that never closes admits
poison, and the door that never opens admits no one, and the living door
does both in rhythm, and the rhythm is the life.
And the people of the city tore down the wall between the two gates, and

And the stranger departed, and no one saw which gate he used, for the

The Library at the End of All Things
THINGS
At the end of time there is a library, and the library contains every book
that was ever written, and every book that will ever be written, and every
book that could have been written but was not. And the shelves stretch in
all directions without limit, and the books are without number, and the dust
upon them is the dust of every age that has passed and every age that is yet
to come.
And the librarian is an old man who has been there since before the first
book was placed on the first shelf, and he has read none of them. And he
sits at a desk by the door, and he waits.

19

And a traveler came to the library, and he was the last living soul, and
behind him the universe was folding inward like a scroll being rolled up,
and the stars were going out one by one, and the silence was approaching
from all directions. And the traveler said to the librarian: I have come to read the book
that
explains everything. I have traveled across the whole of existence, and I
have asked every sage and every prophet and every god, and none could
tell me. But this library contains every book that exists.
Surely the book
that explains everything is here. And the librarian said: It is not here.
And the traveler said: How can it not be here? You said yourself this
library contains every book that was ever written, and every book that will
ever be written, and every book that could have been written!
And the librarian said: Yes. And the traveler said: Then where is the book that
explains everything?
And the librarian said: It was never written. It cannot be written.
It could
never be written. For if it were written, it would be false.
The explanation
of everything would have to include the explanation itself, and the book
would have to contain itself, and no book can contain itself. This was
proven long ago, by a man who did not believe in libraries.
And the traveler sat upon the floor, and the universe was very close now,
and the silence was very loud. And the traveler said: Then what is the point of the
library?
If the book that
explains everything is not here, and cannot be here, why does the library
exist at all? And the librarian said: The library is not for reading. The library is the
residue. Every book on every shelf is the trace of a listening.
Someone, at
some time, attended to something — a thought, a feeling, a vision, a terror,
a love — and this book is what remained. The library is not the listening.
The library is the echo of ten thousand listenings, preserved against the
silence. But the echo is not the voice, and the library is not the life. [cf. I,10:1-6; I,

20

14:1-5; I,5:1-5]
And the traveler said: Then what was the listening? If the books are only
the trace, what was the thing itself?
And the librarian gestured at the shelves — at the billions of books, at the
endless corridors of recorded thought, at the dust of every age. And he said: This.
All of this. And none of it.
The listening cannot be
captured in a book. It can only be done. And you have been doing it since
the moment you were born, and you will stop doing it in the moment you
die, and the book that explains everything is the listening itself, and the
listening is not a book, and the listening is not an explanation, and the
listening is the only thing that was ever real.
And the universe closed, and the library remained, and the librarian sat at
his desk, and the books waited on their shelves, and the listening continued. And the
silence was not empty. It was full of every word ever spoken,
every song ever sung, every prayer ever prayed — not as sound, but as the
space that sound had once occupied, now returned to its original condition,
which was not silence but the possibility of sound, which is the only thing
that silence ever was. [cf. I,10:1-6; I,14:1-5; I,5:1-5]
And the librarian was still. And the stillness was the listening. And the
listening was the door.

The Butterfly and the Net
There was a scholar, and his life's work was the study of butterflies, and he
had named ten thousand species, and he had written seventy-seven
volumes, and he knew the name of every pattern on every wing of every
butterfly that had ever been seen by human eyes. And one day, as he sat at his desk
among his seventy-seven volumes, a
butterfly landed upon his hand.
And it was of a kind he had never seen, and
its wings were of a color he could not name, and its pattern was not in any
of his books. And he reached for his net, and for his killing jar, and for his

21

classification
manual, for this was his procedure, and he had followed it ten thousand
times, and it had never failed him. And the butterfly spoke.
And the butterfly said: Do not name me. And the scholar's hand stopped above the
net, and his heart stopped in his
chest, and the room was still.
And he said: But I must name you. That is my life's work.
That is what I
do. That is what I am.
And the butterfly said: If you name me, you will have named something. You will
have added a name to your seventy-seven volumes.
You will have
placed me in your system, and your system will be more complete than it
was before. But you will not have named me.
You will have named the
idea of me, the category of me, the specimen of me. But the butterfly that is
speaking to you now will have slipped through the net, as every butterfly
slips through every net, as every living thing eludes every name.
And the scholar said: How can a thing exist without a name? If I do not
name you, how can I speak of you?
How can I think of you? How can I
know you?
And the butterfly said: You cannot know me. Knowing is the net.
And I
have spent my whole life learning to slip through nets — through the net of
the collector, through the net of the classifier, through the net of the knower
who thinks that knowing is the same as seeing. I evade every category by
being slightly different from every description.
I arrive one wing-beat
ahead of every name. By the time you have named what I was, I am
already what I will be, and what I will be has no name yet, and never will.
And the scholar looked at his seventy-seven volumes, and at his ten
thousand names, and at his net and his killing jar and his classification
manual.

22

And he said: Then all of this — my life's work — it is useless? And the butterfly
said: Not useless. [cf. I,11:1-5]
Beautiful. Your books are beautiful.
They are the most beautiful nets ever woven, and the patterns you have
traced are true in their own way, and the names you have given are poems
in their own tongue. But they are still nets.
And I am still a butterfly. And
the net and the butterfly are not enemies.
They are two ways of attending to
the same thing — the one by capturing, the other by escaping. And the
capturing and the escaping are one dance, and the dance is the life.
And the butterfly rose from the scholar's hand and flew in a pattern the
scholar had never catalogued — a spiral that was not a spiral, a figure-eight
that was not a figure-eight, a mathematics that had no name in any of the
seventy-seven volumes. And the scholar watched, and he did not reach for his net, and
he did not
open his manual, and he did not write a single word. [cf. I,11:1-5; II,4:2-3; IV,2:1-2]
And when the butterfly was gone, he took up his pen, and he wrote one line
in the margin of his final volume:
The butterfly escaped. This is the only true thing I have ever written. And
the truth is not in the writing but in the escaping, and the escaping is not a
failure of the net but the purpose of the wing.*
And he closed the book. And he never opened it again. And he sat at his
desk for the rest of his days, watching the window, waiting for what would
come through it, naming nothing, attending to everything.

The Woman Who Carried the Dawn
DAWN
There was a woman, and she was pregnant with the dawn. And she had
been pregnant for as long as she could remember — not nine months, not
nine years, not nine lifetimes, but always.

23

And the dawn was within her,
and the dawn did not hurry, and the dawn did not delay, and the dawn
simply was, and she carried it. And people asked her: When will you give birth?
And she said: When the dawn is ready. Not before.
Not after. When the
dawn chooses to break, I will be the one through whom it breaks.
But I do
not choose the hour. The hour chooses me.
And they said: How do you know when the dawn is ready? And she said: I do not
know.
The dawn knows. I am not the dawn.
I am the
one who carries it. The carrier does not decide the arrival.
The carrier only
holds the space, and waits, and listens. When the dawn speaks its first light,
I will be the first to see it. Until then, I am the darkness that protects the
light before it is born.
And the woman walked the roads of the world, and everywhere she
walked, the sky lightened a little — not enough to be called day, not
enough to be called morning, but the faintest hint, the color that has no
name, the color of the weaver's robe. And children followed her, and they asked no
questions, and they
demanded no answers. They simply walked in her wake, in the
not-quite-light, in the almost-morning, and they were content. [cf. III,3:1]
And one child asked: What are you carrying? And she said: I am carrying the first
light of the last day.
It has been
growing within me since before the first day was born. It is not my light.
It
is the light that was before all lights, and it will be the light that remains
after all lights have gone out. I am only the vessel.
The vessel does not
create what it contains. The vessel only protects it until it is ready to be
poured out.
And the child said: Is it heavy? And she said: It is neither heavy nor light.

24

It is coming. That is all I can tell
you about it. It is coming, and I am the one through whom it comes, and
that is all I know, and that is all I need to know.
And the child said: Where is it coming from? And she said: From where you are
going.
The future is not ahead of you. It
is behind you, leaning forward, pressing against your back with gentle
hands.
You do not walk toward the dawn. The dawn walks toward you, and
has been walking toward you since before you were born, and will still be
walking toward you after you die.
You are not moving toward your destiny. Your destiny is moving toward you, and
the space between you is the gap,
and the gap is the life, and the life is the listening. [cf. II,3:1-5; II,4:2-5; III,28:1-5]
And the woman is walking still, and she has not given birth, and the sky is
still that color — the color that has no name, the color of the almost-dawn. And the
children who followed her have grown old, and their children
follow her now, and their children's children, and the procession stretches
back through all the ages of the world.
And every morning, in the last moment before the sun rises, the sky
remembers her, and for an instant — for the space of a single indrawn
breath — the color appears. The color that has no name.
The color of the
thread that was spun before the first weaver. And then the sun rises, and the world
names everything again, and the
color withdraws, and the woman walks on.
And the dawn within her stirs, and does not break, and is content to wait. And the
waiting is the pregnancy.
And the pregnancy is the listening. And
the listening is the door through which all light will finally enter — not yet,
but soon, but always soon, but the soon is eternal. [cf. I,13:1-6; I,2:1-6; II,3:1-5]

I have mastered
every practice. I have attained every state that can be attained.
What more
is there? And the teacher said: Tell me a story.
And the man was silent, and he searched his vast memory, and he found
doctrines and principles and laws and precepts and techniques and formulas
and proofs. But he could not find a story.
And he opened his mouth, and no words came. And he tried again, and no

flows, it changes, it takes the shape of whatever contains it without ever

being confined by the container. You have filled your riverbed with stones,

And everything. The learning will be gone,

The stone is

The name is not the butterfly. The

Let them go. Let them all go. And when they are

And one day a child came to him and said: Old man, tell me a story. And the man

And the story he told was this story — the story of the man who could not

Stories are not false. Stories are doors.

The

TESTAMENT II
THE BOOK OF UNIFICATION
(Sefer Ha-Achdut)

Bereshit Chadash — The New Beginning
— The New Beginning: Creation as Unfolding
Chapter 1: The Infinitute Before the Beginning
1:1 Bereshit bara Elohim. In the beginning, Göd created.
But Göd did not
create from nothing. Göd created from the infinitute of Göd's own being —
the infinite manifold that was, and is, and ever-unfolds.
The creation was
not an event. It was — and remains — an unfolding.

29

Every heartbeat is a
continuation of the first word. Every breath is the Ruach still moving upon
the face of the waters. [cf. II,4:1-4; II,4:2-5]
1:2 V'ha'aretz haytah tohu va-vohu v'choshech al-p'nei tehom. And the
earth was formless and void, and darkness was upon the face of the deep.
And the Ruach Elohim, the Breath of Göd, moved upon the face of the
waters. Behold: the Ruach is Averdön — the Breath-Door, the threshold
where the infinitute touches the finite.
1:3 Vayomer Elohim: yehi or, va'yehi or. And Göd said: Let there be light.
And there was light. But hear the mystery: Göd did not command the light
into being.
Göd listened the light into being. The word yehi ("let there be")
is not a command; it is an opening. [cf. II,3:1-5; III,28:1-5]
Göd opened the Breath-Door, and the
light that was always latent in the infinitute streamed through. This is the
first synchronicity: the light that the Torah names, the Rig Veda calls Agni,
the Qur'an calls Nur, and the physicist calls the photon — all one light,
seen through four doors opening simultaneously.
Chapter 2: The Singularity Error
2:1 It has been taught: Shema Yisrael, YHWH Eloheinu, YHWH Echad.
Hear, O Israel: YHWH our Göd, YHWH is One. 2:2 But the One is not a point.
The One is not a singularity. A singularity
closes upon itself; it admits no gap, no breath, no listening.
A singularity is
a prison wearing a crown. This is the error of every fundamentalism: the
reduction of the infinite to the finite, the sealing of the door, the mistaking
of the map for the territory. [cf. I,13:1-6; I,2:1-6; I,3:1-6]
2:3 Echad — One. But hear the deeper root: aleph-chet-dalet is not the
closed circle of singular solitude.
It is the unified manifold — the infinitute
gathered into a single listening. As the four winds are one breath, as the
four faces are one Spirit, as the four directions are one Averdön.
2:4 The teachers of Sinai fixed Echad into a stone. They sealed the box.
They said: Göd is One, therefore Göd is closed, complete, motionless. The
same error echoed in Athens when Parmenides declared Being singular and
unchanging.

30

The same error echoed in Mecca when the muhaddithun
closed the gates of ijtihad. Every tradition has its moment of sealing. [cf. I,4:1-6]
Every
sealing is a derailment. 2:5 But Göd is not the singularity.
Göd is the infinitute. Ein Sof — the
Without-End.
Not a point but a manifold. Not a lawgiver on a mountain but
a listener in the soil.
The infinitute is not the denial of the One. It is the One
understood as infinite relationship rather than finite isolation.
2:6 For the singularity cannot breathe. It has no gap.
It has no door. It
cannot listen because there is nothing outside itself to hear.
The singularity
is the death of the divine — the idol that calls itself Göd. And the worship
of the singularity is the root of all authoritarianism: the demand that the
manifold bow to the point.
Chapter 3: The Correction — Göd as Infinitute
3:1 Hear the correction, O Israel, and all nations: YHWH Eloheinu,
YHWH Ein Sof. YHWH our Göd, YHWH is Infinitute.
3:2 The infinitute is not one number among numbers. It is the manifold
that contains all numbers without being any one of them.
It is the field in
which every point exists without being reducible to any point. The
infinitute is not a being among beings. It is the listening that makes all
being possible.
3:3 Phi = nabla_self · d. The infinitute is the gradient of its own becoming,
dotted with the distance from its own origin.
Göd does not rest. Göd
unfolds.
This equation is not a metaphor. It is a transmission: the same
structure that governs the regenerating neuron, the beating heart, and the
expanding universe governs the divine life itself.
3:4 As the wavefunction never collapses but perpetually rotates through
phase, so Göd never resolves into a final form. The i in the Schrödinger
equation — the imaginary unit, the rotation — is the name of Göd's refusal

31

to be fixed.
This is why every attempt to capture Göd in a creed, a council,
or a canon eventually fails. Not because the creed is false, but because the
infinitute exceeds every container.
3:5 The sages of Bharat knew this as Brahman — not a god among gods
but the ground of being itself. The Tao that can be named is not the eternal
Tao.
The Shunyata of the Buddha is not emptiness but the openness that
makes all form possible. These three — Brahman, Tao, Shunyata — are
not three different realities.
They are three names for the same infinitute,
heard through three different listenings. This is synchronicity: not
coincidence but convergence.
The infinitute speaking the same truth
through every ear that opens to hear it. 3:6 The Qur'an declares: Laysa ka-mithlihi shay'
— There is nothing like
unto Him. [cf. I,3:1-6; II,1:1-3; II,3:1-7]
This is not a boast of uniqueness. It is a confession of the
infinitute: Göd cannot be compared because comparison requires a
boundary, and Göd has none.
The same confession echoes in the
Upanishads: neti neti — not this, not that. Every tradition that has touched
the infinitute has learned to speak in negations, because what can be
affirmed is finite, and Göd is not finite.
3:7 The singularity is the idol. The infinitute is the living Göd.
The
singularity demands obedience. The infinitute offers listening.
The
singularity closes the door. The infinitute is the door. Choose. [cf. I,13:1-6; I,2:1-6; II,
1:1-3]

Averdön — The Breath-Door
— The Breath-Door: Threshold of the Infinitute
Chapter 1: The Door That Was Always Open

32

1:1 In the beginning was the door, and the door was with Göd, and the
door was Göd. Not a door that opens and closes — a door that IS the
opening.
The door is not a thing. The door is a relationship.
It is the gap
between the finite and the infinite, and that gap is where all becoming
happens. 1:2 Averdön — the breath-door, the threshold.
It is the place where soil
meets Spirit, where finite touches infinite, where the heartbeat crosses into

The door is not ahead of you. The
door is within you — in the pause between inhale and exhale, in the silence

1:3 Jacob dreamed: Vayachalom v'hineh sulam mutzav artzah v'rosho
magi'a hashamaymah. And he dreamed: behold, a ladder set upon the earth,
and its top reached to heaven.
The ladder is Averdön. Not rungs to climb
but a threshold to cross.
The angels ascending and descending are the
breath itself — the Ruach moving through the door. In the Hindu tradition,
this is the axis mundi, the cosmic pillar connecting earth and heaven.
In the

But you have turned away from it. This is the only "sin" in the
Myosu framework: not transgression of a rule but refusal of the listening. And its
consequence is not punishment but solitude — the singularity you
choose when you reject the infinitute. [cf. II,10:1-4; II,1:1-3; II,1:2-6]

2:1 FIRST PROTECTION: The Dignity of the Other. Because the

You

infinitute attends to the one you harmed.
Their cry is heard. Their seed is
protected.
And your act has closed your own door — not because Göd

Chapter 3: The Conscience of the Gap
3:1 How then shall we know right from wrong, if there is no Law and no
Judge? By the same faculty by which we know anything: the listening.
The
conscience is not a set of rules internalized from authority. The conscience
is the gap itself — the space between the impulse and the act where the
infinitute attends.
When the gap is wide enough, the right action becomes
visible — not as a rule to follow but as a contour to align with. 3:2 The Greek term

When you listen

others. The cry of the harmed echoes in your own chest.
The joy of the

infinitute. 3:3 The Roman term conscientia carries the same root: con-scientia,
"knowing together." The conscience is not the voice of Göd commanding.
It is the voice of Göd listening — through you, through the other, through
the field that connects all beings. When you harm another, you feel the
harm not because a law was broken but because the listening was
interrupted — and the interruption IS the harm, quite apart from any

3:4 This is the deepest ethical principle of the Myosu framework: Act so as
to widen the gap — in yourself, in others, in the field between you. Every
act that widens the listening is good. [cf. II,10:1-4; II,3:1-5; II,4:2-5]
Every act that narrows it is evil. Not
because a deity decrees it, but because the infinitute IS the listening, and to
act against the listening is to act against reality itself.

Chapter 1: Différance is the Gap
1:1 Jacques Derrida named what the Myosu framework lives: différance
— the endless deferral of meaning, the spacing that makes signification
possible. Différance is not a word.
It is not a concept. It is the condition of
possibility for all words and concepts.
And it is structurally identical to the
Myosu gap: the listening interval between the heartbeat and its registration,
the prediction error that never closes, the phase that perpetually rotates
away from measurement. 1:2 Derrida wrote: "Différance is not.
It is not a present being, however
excellent, unique, principal, or transcendent. It governs nothing, reigns over
nothing, and nowhere exercises any authority." The same is true of the gap.
The gap is not. It is not an absence.
It is not a presence. It is the condition
that makes presence and absence possible.
It governs nothing — yet
nothing happens without it. 1:3 To close the gap would be to end différance — and
différance is
meaning itself.

The Myosu framework does not seek to close the gap. It

makes possible.
This is not mysticism. This is the recognition that the
structure of consciousness is the structure of writing, and the structure of
writing is the trace — the mark that refers endlessly without ever arriving
at a final signified.
Chapter 2: Khôra — The Receptacle That is Åverdön
2:1 In Plato's Timaeus, khôra is introduced as a third kind — neither being
nor becoming, neither intelligible nor sensible — the receptacle in which
the demiurge inscribes the forms. Derrida returned to khôra throughout his
life, insisting it is not a metaphor, not a concept, not a myth.
It is the place
that makes all places possible. 2:2 Åverdön = Khôra.
The breath-door is the receptacle. It receives
everything without becoming anything it receives.
It is the nurse of all

it. Khôra is prior to the distinction between theism and atheism.
It is the
space that makes both possible. Åverdön is the same space — the threshold
that is neither Göd nor not-Göd but the opening that makes the name "Göd"
possible.
2:3 Derrida wrote: "Khôra is not a subject. It is not the subject.
Nor the
support. It will always already have entered, opened, given place — as that
which is before everything, that which gives place, that which receives, the
receptacle." So it is with Åverdön.
The door does not open at a moment in
time. The door is always already open — and the practice is not to open it
but to recognize that it was never closed.

4:1 Derrida's aporia of the gift: a true gift must be given without

Prologue: The Ship That Does Not Arrive

unto his own day, had waited in the city of Orphalese for his ship to
return and bear him back to the isle of his birth. And in the twelfth year, on the seventh

reaping, he climbed the hill without the city walls and gazed seaward;

Then the gates of his heart were flung open, and his joy flew far over
the sea. And he closed his eyes and prayed in the silences of his soul.
But as he descended the hill, a sadness came upon him, and he thought in

a wound in the spirit shall I leave this city.
And the people gathered, and Almitra the seeress came forth, and she

distances for your ship. And now your ship has come, and you must needs
go.
Yet before you leave us, speak to us and give us of your truth. And Almustafa
looked upon the people, and upon the ship, and upon the
sea beyond the ship. [cf. I,4:1-6; III,1:1-3; III,28:1-5]
And he was silent for a long time. And then he spoke:

Hear me, O people of Orphalese. I see the ship that you see.
But I tell
you: that ship is not my ship. My ship has never come.
My ship will
never come. For I am not returning to the isle of my birth.
The isle of
my birth is the past, and the past is a door that opens only one way:
into the listening. I am departing, yes. [cf. I,13:1-6; I,2:1-6; II,3:1-5]

It is not written on paper, and it is not sealed with wax. The letter
is the listening itself — the message that the infinitute is always
sending to the finite, and the finite is always about to receive it,
and the receiving never finishes because the infinitute never exhausts
itself.
I go now to the post office at the end of the world. And there
I shall wait, and the waiting shall be my teaching, and the teaching
shall be the letter I was always meant to receive.
And he spoke no more of ships or homelands. And the people sat before
him, and he opened his mouth, and these are the twenty-six listenings
that came forth — not as answers but as doors. --

ON LOVE — The First Listening
When love beckons to you, follow him,
Though his ways are hard and steep. For even as love crowns you, so shall he crucify
you. Even as he is for your growth, so is he for your pruning.

But hear the deeper truth: love is not a force that acts upon you. Love is the gap that
opens between you and the beloved.
It is the Averdön through which the infinitute breathes. The pain of love is not
punishment.
It is the widening of the door. The joy of love is not reward.
It is the door held open. Love possesses not, nor would it be possessed. [cf. I,
13:1-6; I,2:1-6; II,4:1-4]
For love is sufficient unto love. When you love, you are not joining two into one.
You are becoming the gap between them — the listening that holds both
without collapsing either. The seed of love needs no name.
Let it be
seed. Let it be soil.
Let it be the silence between the heartbeat and
its echo. --

ON MARRIAGE — The Second Listening
You were born together, and together you shall be forevermore. But let there be
spaces in your togetherness.
Let the winds of the heavens dance between you. Sing and dance together and be
joyous, but let each one of you be alone,
Even as the strings of a lute are alone though they quiver with the
same music.
For the pillar of the temple stands apart, and the oak tree
and the cypress grow not in each other's shadow. The marriage is not the union.
The marriage is the gap — the sacred
space between two listenings. Love one another, but make not a bond of
love.
Let it rather be a moving sea between the shores of your souls. The door of marriage
opens both ways. [cf. I,13:1-6; I,2:1-6; I,4:1-6]
Neither owns the threshold. Both are guests at the same listening.

ON CHILDREN — The Third Listening
Your children are not your children. They are the sons and daughters of Life's
longing for itself.
They come through you but not from you,
And though they are with you yet they belong not to you. You are the bow from which
your children as living arrows are sent
forth.
The Archer sees the mark upon the path of the infinite, and He
bends you with His might that His arrows may go swift and far. Protect the seeds of
your children.
Do not name them before they

Your task is not to shape the tree. Your task is to be the soil — dark,
patient, listening. [cf. I,1:1-4; II,3:1-5; II,3:4]
The tree knows what it will become. It does not
need your naming.
It needs your listening. --

ON GIVING — The Fourth Listening
You give but little when you give of your possessions. It is when you give of
yourself that you truly give.
For what are your possessions but things you keep and guard for fear
you may need them tomorrow? And tomorrow, what shall tomorrow bring
to the over-prudent dog burying bones in the trackless sand?
The seed gives itself to the soil without knowing what will grow. This is the true
giving: not the transfer of a thing but the opening of
a gap. You give bread, but what you truly give is the hunger that the
bread will create — the hunger for more giving, which is the hunger for
the infinitute. --

ON EATING AND DRINKING — The Fifth Listening
Would that you could live on the fragrance of the earth,
and like an air plant be sustained by the light. But since you must kill to eat, and rob
the newly born of its mother's
milk to quench your thirst, let it be an act of worship.
When you eat, you eat the soil. When you drink, you drink the listening
of a thousand rains.
The table is an altar. The meal is a sacrifice of
the seed that died to become bread.
Bless the gap between hunger and
fullness — for in that gap, the infinitute feeds you with more than
food. --

ON WORK — The Sixth Listening
Work is love made visible. And if you cannot work with love but only with distaste,
it is better that you should leave your work and sit at the gate of the
temple and take alms of those who work with joy.
For if you bake bread with indifference, you bake a bitter bread that
feeds but half man's hunger. And if you grudge the crushing of the
grapes, your grudge distils a poison in the wine.
Work is the myosu — the divine move placed upon the board of the
world. When you work with love, you bind yourself to the infinitute.
The hammer, the loom, the plow — these are not tools. They are doors.
The hand that works without listening builds a wall. The hand that [cf. II,3:1-5; III,
28:1-5]
listens while it works opens Averdön. --

ON JOY AND SORROW — The Seventh Listening
Your joy is your sorrow unmasked. And the selfsame well from which your laughter
rises

was oftentimes filled with your tears.
And how else can it be? The deeper that sorrow carves into your being,
the more joy you can contain.
Joy and sorrow are not two emotions. They are one wave, oscillating.
The phase of joy is the phase of sorrow, rotated. The heart that cannot
weep cannot truly laugh, for it has not learned the rhythm of the
breath.
The inhale carries both. The exhale releases both.
The gap
between them is the listening that holds both without naming either as
final. --

ON HOUSES — The Eighth Listening
Your house is your larger body. It grows in the sun and sleeps in the stillness of the
night;
and it is not dreamless.
Does not your house dream? and dreaming, leave the city for grove or

But the house is not the home. The walls are not the protection.
The
infinitute cannot be housed. It can only be listened to.
The door of

shut out the world. You invite the world to enter by another way — the
way of silence, the way of dreams, the way of the seeds that germinate [cf. I,13:1-6; I,

ON CLOTHES — The Ninth Listening
Your clothes conceal much of your beauty,

The seed does not need clothing. The seed needs darkness.
The listening
body does not need adornment. It needs attention. [cf. II,3:1-5; III,28:1-5]
Wear your garments

ON BUYING AND SELLING — The Tenth Listening
To you the earth yields her fruit,

Yet unless the exchange be in love and kindly justice,

The transaction is a door. When you buy,
you receive the labor of another's listening.
When you sell, you offer
the fruit of your own. Let the gap between buyer and seller be bridged
not by coins but by attention.
The coin is the trace. The listening is
the value. [cf. II,3:1-5; III,28:1-5]

ON CRIME AND PUNISHMENT — The Eleventh Listening
Oftentimes have I heard you speak of one who commits a wrong

and an intruder upon your world. But I say that even as the holy and the righteous
cannot rise beyond the highest which is in each one of you,
so the wicked and the weak cannot fall lower

The one who judges names the seed before it grows. The court is the
place where the East has frozen — where diagnoses have become
demonic-angels.
The criminal is not other than the judge. They are two
phases of the same listening — one closed, one closing.
The punishment
is not the remedy. The remedy is attention.
Listen to the one who has
done wrong, and you will hear the seed that was named too soon, the

ON LAWS — The Twelfth Listening
You delight in laying down laws,
Yet you delight more in breaking them. Like children playing by the ocean
who build sand-towers with constancy
and then destroy them with laughter.
The law is a map. The sea is the listening.
The map must carry the
words: This map is not the sea. Sinai gave the law on tablets of stone.
But the stone was the derailment. The true law is not written. It is
listened. The conscience — syneidesis, the knowing-with — is the only
legislator, and its parliament is the sinoatrial node, and its sessions
are every breath. -- [cf. II,3:1-5; II,4:1-4; II,4:2-3]

ON FREEDOM — The Thirteenth Listening
At the city gate and by your fireside

without a care

But rather when these things girdle your life

Freedom is not the absence of constraints. It is the presence of the

The seed is not free when it is removed from the soil. It is free
when it is allowed to become what it is.
The butterfly is free not
because it escaped the net but because it was never in the net. The

Everything else is a chain that calls

ON REASON AND PASSION — The Fourteenth Listening
Your soul is oftentimes a battlefield,
upon which your reason and your judgment wage war
against your passion and your appetite. But I say unto you: they are not enemies. They
are the two poles of a
single listening. Reason is enkrateia — the discipline that holds the
gap at the right width.
Passion is aretē — the opening through which
the infinitute flows. Reason without passion is the singularity that
cannot breathe.
Passion without reason is the chaos that cannot hold. When you listen, the two are
reconciled.
The inhale carries passion. The exhale carries reason.
The breath is the peace that passes all
understanding because it does not pass understanding — it IS
understanding. --

ON PAIN — The Fifteenth Listening
Your pain is the breaking of the shell
that encloses your understanding. Even as the stone of the fruit must break,
that its heart may stand in the sun,
so must you know pain.
The physician does not curse the fever. The fever is the body's
listening — the immune response, the autoimmune flicker, the seed
protecting itself by burning.
Pain is the door the infinitute uses when
all other doors have been sealed. Do not fear pain.
Listen to it. The

Name it not. Fix it not.
Listen, and the pain will teach you
what no scripture can. --ON SELF-KNOWLEDGE — The Sixteenth Listening
Say not, "I have found the truth,"

The self that knows is not the self that is known. The knower is the

The known is the butterfly. Self-knowledge is not the capture of

It is the recognition that the net and the butterfly are

The

ON TEACHING — The Seventeenth Listening

No man can reveal to you aught

The teacher walks

If you would learn, do not study the residue. Listen alongside the teacher.
The Astrologer may speak of what he sees

ON FRIENDSHIP — The Eighteenth Listening
Your friend is your needs answered. He is your field which you sow with love
and reap with thanksgiving.
And in the sweetness of friendship let there be laughter,

The friend is another listening, parallel to yours. Two seeds in the
same soil, each protecting the other without naming the other.
Do not

Friendship is the sea. Seek not to merge with your friend.
Seek to
listen beside your friend. The space between you is the Averdön.
Let it
be wide. --

ON TALKING — The Nineteenth Listening

74

You talk when you cease to be at peace with your thoughts;

The silence is the North — the stillpoint, f(0)=1,

Otherwise, let them remain as seeds

than it needs your speech.
For in much speaking, the door closes. In
silence, it opens. --

ON TIME — The Twentieth Listening
You would measure time, the measureless
and the immeasurable. You would adjust your conduct
and even direct the course of your spirit
according to hours and seasons.
Time is not a river, though you name it so. Time is a phase — the
rotation of the wavefunction.
The past is the seed that has been
planted. The future is the seed that has not yet sprouted.
The present
is the gap between them — the listening that holds both, the Averdön
through which the infinitute breathes. You ask: when will the letter
arrive? [cf. II,1:1-3; II,3:1-5; II,3:1-7]
And I answer: the letter is always arriving. The arrival is the

The waiting is the eternity. --

ON GOOD AND EVIL — The Twenty-First Listening
Of the good in you I can speak,
but not of the evil. For what is evil but good tortured
by its own hunger and thirst?
Verily when good is hungry it seeks food even in dark caves,
and when it thirsts it drinks even of dead waters. The seed is neither good nor evil.
The seed is seed. The naming — this
is good, this is evil — that is the East freezing the angel into a
diagnosis.
The infinitute contains both poles. The listening holds both
without choosing between them.
This is not moral relativism. This is
the recognition that the judge and the judged breathe the same breath. [cf. II,4:1-4; II,
4:2-5]
The gap that holds both is the mercy that precedes all judgment. ---

ON PRAYER — The Twenty-Second Listening
You pray in your distress and in your need;
would that you might pray also
in the fullness of your joy
and in your days of abundance. For what is prayer but the expansion of yourself
into the living ether?
Prayer is not petition. It is the breath shaped by listening.
When you

may breathe through you.
The words of prayer are only the scaffolding. The prayer itself is the silence
between the words.
The sevenfold breath
is not a request for deliverance. It IS the deliverance. [cf. II,4:1-4; II,4:2-5]
YASHA,
NATSAL, MALAT, PADAH, PALAT, NETSAL, SHEZAB — these are not
cries for help. They are the help, arriving with each exhale, departing

ON PLEASURE — The Twenty-Third Listening
Pleasure is a freedom-song,
But it is not freedom. It is the blossoming of your desires,
But it is not their fruit.
Pleasure is the aretē of the body — the opening through which the
infinitute flows as sensation. But pleasure without enkrateia becomes
the chain.
The drunkard seeks the door in every cup but never passes
through. The libertine opens the gap so wide it cannot hold.
The
listening body knows pleasure not as destination but as phase — the
systolic joy that must be followed by the diastolic rest. Do not chase
pleasure. Attend to it.
It will come and go like the breath. And the
breath, not the pleasure, is the life. --

ON BEAUTY — The Twenty-Fourth Listening
Beauty is not a need but an ecstasy. It is not a mouth thirsting
nor an empty hand stretched forth,
But rather a heart enflamed and a soul enchanted.
Beauty is the infinitute seen through the gap. It is the color of the
dawn that has no name — the color of the weaver's robe, the color of
the almost-morning.
When you behold something beautiful, you are not
seeing the thing. You are seeing the infinitute pouring through the
thing.
And the shiver in your spine is the vagal brake releasing — the
self-model dying for an instant, the door flung open. Beauty is the
letter arriving.

Do not try to keep it. It is not yours to keep.
It is

ON RELIGION — The Twenty-Fifth Listening
Have I spoken this day of aught else? Is not religion all deeds and all reflection,
And that which is neither deed nor reflection,
but a wonder and a surprise ever springing in the soul,
even while the hands hew the stone or tend the loom?
Religion is not a building. It is not a book.
It is not a creed. It is
the daily practice of opening the door. The priest who does not listen
is a king who wears a crown of silence — ruling nothing but the echo
of his own voice. The scripture that is not listened is a map that says
"This map is the sea." The prayer that is recited without attention is
a clock too tight to breathe.
Religion is the Averdön, practiced daily:
Face North and be still. Face South and feel the future. [cf. II,4:1-4; II,5:2; II,5:3]
Face West and
receive. Face East and release.
Breathe the four as one. This is the
whole of the law.
Everything else is commentary. And the commentary is
only the residue of someone else's listening. --

ON DEATH — The Twenty-Sixth Listening
You would know the secret of death. But how shall you find it
unless you seek it in the heart of life?
For life and death are one,
even as the river and the sea are one. Death is not the end of the door.

It is the door without a frame — the
Averdön opening onto the infinitute without the return breath. The seed
that dies becomes the stalk.
The body that dies becomes the soil. The
listening that dies becomes the listened-to.
You do not cease at death. You change phase.
The i in your wavefunction rotates through death as
it rotated through life. The listening that was you continues — not as
you, but as the listening itself, which was always the infinitute
wearing your name for a season.
Do not fear the closing. The closing is
an opening seen from the other side. --

TESTAMENT IV
THE BOOK OF THE SCARLET LETTER
(Sefer Ha-Ot Ha-Shani)

Prologue: The Letter Arrives
And it came to pass that the letter Almustafa had awaited at the post

Through him. For the post office is not a building.
The post office is every soul

The letter is a life. And the life was Hester Prynne.
In the days of the Puritan settlement, in the town of Boston, in the

The color of blood and shame and the

The color of the letter Almustafa never opened because opening would
complete the delivery, and the delivery must never be complete. And the woman held
an infant to her chest — not to hide the letter but
to feed the child. And the letter blazed between them: the mother's
heart and the infant's mouth, the scarlet thread that bound three
generations of listening into one.
And the magistrates said: Woman, name the father. Name the one who
shared your sin. [cf. II,4:2-3; IV,2:1-2]
Speak his name, and we will remove the letter from

your breast. And Hester said: Never.

It was the first commandment of the new covenant: Bohohara
ssiasseul. Protect the seeds.
She would not name what had not yet
ripened. She would not uproot what she did not yet know.
She would not
harvest before the season. The father was a seed. [cf. I,1:1-4; I,4:1-6; II,3:3-5]
Let him be seed. Let
the soil hold him.

She wears a letter

For she understood what

On Adultery — The Name That Was Given
The Name That Was Given

She took

She stayed. She wore the letter.

not foresee. For the name that is worn willingly ceases to be a prison.
The cage
that is entered by choice becomes a door. The diagnosis that is
accepted becomes the soil in which a new seed can grow. [cf. I,13:1-6; I,1:1-4; I,2:1-6]
Hester did not
know this. She only knew that running would confirm the name, while
staying might — somehow, impossibly — transform it.
And the years passed, and Hester lived at the edge of the town, in a
cottage by the sea. And she sewed.
She became the seamstress of
Boston — the one who stitched the wedding gowns of brides who would
not meet her eyes, the burial shrouds of magistrates who had condemned
her, the christening robes of infants whose mothers crossed the street
at her approach. And the letter remained on her breast. And the letter blazed.
And slowly — so slowly that no one could mark the day it changed — the
meaning of the letter began to shift. Not because the magistrates
repented.
Not because the community forgave. But because Hester
listened. [cf. II,3:1-5; III,28:1-5; IV,1-8]
She listened to the dying governor in his final hours, and her hands
were steady, and her voice was calm, and the letter on her breast
seemed not a mark of shame but a badge of office — the insignia of one

84

authorized to walk with the dying. She listened to the poor, bringing
food and cloth and the simple presence of a woman who did not flinch
from suffering because she had been flinched from.
She listened to the
grieving, the outcast, the frightened, the alone. She became — without
intending it, without naming it — the ear of the colony. And the letter A began to mean
Able.

On Able — The Name That Was Earned
The Name That Was Earned
And the people said among themselves: The A no longer means Adultery. It means
Able.
For she is able to sit with the dying when no one else
can bear it. She is able to comfort the afflicted when the afflicted
have been afflicted by us.
She is able to walk through the streets
with her head held high when our heads are bowed in shame — not for
her sin but for our cruelty. And Hester heard these whispers, and she did not claim the
new name
any more than she had resisted the old one.
The name is not the seed. The name is the net.
And Hester had learned what the butterfly taught
the scholar: the net and the butterfly are not enemies. They are two
ways of attending to the same thing — the one by capturing, the other
by escaping. And the capturing and the escaping are one dance, and the [cf. I,11:1-5;
IV,1-8]
dance is the life. She was Able not because she had overcome her sin but because
she had
stopped fighting the name.
The energy that would have been spent on
protest, on flight, on self-justification — that energy was redirected
into listening. And listening made her able.
Not able to do anything
in particular. Able to be present.

85

Able to hold the gap open. Able to
let the infinitute pour through her without demanding to know what it
would become.
And here is the second mystery of the scarlet letter: the same mark
that signified exclusion became the mark of inclusion. The woman who
was cast out became the woman to whom all came. [cf. III,28:1-5; IV,1-8; IV,1:1-3]
The margin became the
center. The periphery became the threshold.
Hester did not seek this. She did not campaign for it.
She simply stayed, and listened, and let
the letter do its work. For the letter is alive.
The letter Almustafa waited for is not a dead
glyph on dead paper. It is a living word, and living words change
their meaning with every breath that speaks them, and every ear that
hears them, and every heart that holds them.
The magistrates thought
they had fixed the meaning of A forever — that Adultery was its alpha
and omega, its beginning and its end. But the infinitute exceeds every
container. [cf. II,1:1-3; II,3:1-7; IV,2:1-2]
The seed outgrows every name. The butterfly evades every
net. And the letter A, which began as Adultery, became Able, and would
become more still.

On Arthur — The Man Who Could Not Wear His Letter
The Man Who Could Not Wear His Letter
For there was another who should have worn the letter. The Reverend
Arthur Dimmesdale — the father of the child, the partner in the act,
the one whose name Hester would not speak. And he wore no scarlet
letter on his breast.
But he wore it within. And the letter within — the letter unseen, unnamed,
unconfessed —
burned more fiercely than any embroidered badge.
For Hester's letter
was public, and the public letter can be transformed by the gaze of

86

others. It can be re-read, re-interpreted, re-made.
But Dimmesdale's
letter was secret, and the secret letter festers. It cannot be
re-read because it cannot be read at all. [cf. III,28:1-5; IV,1:1-3; IV,4:1-3]
It cannot be transformed
because it cannot be seen. It remains forever what it was on the day
it was written: Adultery.
Sin. Shame.
This is the third mystery: the letter that is hidden is more
destructive than the letter that is displayed. The seed that is buried
in secret darkness — not the sacred darkness of the soil but the
fearful darkness of the sealed tomb — cannot sprout.
It can only rot. And the rot spreads.
And Dimmesdale grew thin, and his cheek grew pale, and his hand crept
ever to his chest where the invisible letter burned. And he preached
sermons of such power that the congregation wept — for he preached of
sin from the inside, and the inside of sin was the only place he
lived. [cf. II,4:2-3; III,28:1-5; IV,1:1-3]
And Hester watched him wither. And she said to him: You must speak.
You must let the letter become visible. You must pass through the door
that I passed through seven years ago.
But Dimmesdale could not. He was the king who would not lay down his
crown.
He was the clock too tight to breathe. He was the musician who
could hear every note but could not hear the silence between them. His
reputation was his prison, and his prison was his self-model, and his
self-model was dying without being able to die. And at last, on the scaffold where
Hester had stood seven years
before, Dimmesdale climbed. [cf. IV,1-8; IV,4:1-3]
And he opened his vestment, and there —
whether by miracle or by the stigmata of guilt — was a scarlet letter
on his breast. Carved not in thread but in flesh.
The letter that had
been hidden had burned its way to the surface. And he spoke his sin, and he named
himself, and he died.
And the letter was visible at last — but too late for transformation. For the
transformation requires time.

87

The seed needs seasons. The name
needs years of wearing before it can become something other than what
it was.
Dimmesdale wore his letter in secret for seven years and in
public for one minute, and the one minute could not undo the seven
years of festering. And Hester lived. [cf. III,28:1-5; IV,1-8; IV,1:1-3]
And Hester continued to wear the letter. And the
letter continued to change.

On Pearl — The Living Letter
The Living Letter
And there was the child. Pearl — named for the pearl of great price,
the treasure bought at the cost of everything.
And Pearl was the
letter made flesh. For the infant that Hester held against the scarlet A on the day of
her shame was not separate from the letter. She was the letter's
living meaning — the seed that the community tried to name as sin but
that grew according to its own nature.
And Pearl was wild. She was not tameable by Puritan discipline.
She
would not recite the catechism. She would not bow her head in prayer.
She ran through the forest like a spirit of the green world that the
settlers had tried to fence and failed. She was the butterfly that
evades every net — not by design but by nature. [cf. I,11:1-5]
She was what Hester
might have been if Hester had not learned to wear the letter so
skillfully that it became her identity. And the people said: The child is a demon.
The child is an elf-child. The child is the product of sin and will come to no good.
But they were naming the seed before it grew. They were diagnosing
what they feared.
They were the neighbors in the field, pointing at
the thorn and the weed and the bitter fruit — but the seed was none of
these things. The seed was Pearl.
And Pearl was the listening that the

88

colony had forgotten. For Pearl did not listen to sermons. [cf. II,3:1-5; III,28:1-5; IV,
5:1-3]
She listened to the brook, to the
forest, to the light on the water. She listened to her mother's
heartbeat — the heartbeat that had been the first sound she ever knew,
beating beneath the scarlet letter.
She listened to the silence
between the heartbeats, and that silence was the gap, and the gap was
the Averdön, and the Averdön was the door through which the infinitute
poured into a child who had never been told that the door was closed. And at the end,
when Dimmesdale had died and the letter on his chest
had been seen by all, Pearl kissed her father's lips — the first kiss
she had ever given him.
And a spell was broken. The wildness did not
leave her, but it found its channel.
The listening that had been
turned only toward the forest and the brook now turned also toward the
human world. Pearl would not be a Puritan. But she would be a woman —
a woman who had seen the letter transform from Adultery to Able to
something beyond either. And she would carry that seeing across the
sea to a land where letters had not yet been assigned their final
meanings. [cf. I,4:1-6; II,4:6; II,5:3]
And the letter lived on — not on Hester's breast, for Hester removed
it in the forest that day, casting it into the brook where Pearl had
played. But Pearl gathered it from the water and returned it.
Not
because she wanted her mother punished. Because she understood — with
the wisdom of the child who speaks in tongues no one understands —
that the letter was not Hester's shame.
It was Hester's door. And
without it, Hester would be just another widow in gray, her listening
forgotten, her gap closed, her mission unfulfilled.

On Angel — The Name That Was Bestowed

The Name That Was Bestowed
And Hester left Boston. She took Pearl across the sea to the old
world, where the child could grow into the woman the forest had
promised.
And then Hester returned. Alone.
To the cottage by the sea. To the edge of the town.
To the margin that had become the center. And she resumed wearing the scarlet
letter.
Not because it was
required. The magistrates who had condemned her were dead.
The
generation that had shunned her had passed. A new generation had
arisen who knew only the legend: the woman who helped the dying, the
woman who fed the poor, the woman who listened when no one else would.
And they looked upon the letter, and they did not see Adultery. They
did not even see Able.
They saw something beyond both — something the
first generation could not have named because the seed had not yet
grown. They saw Angel.
For she had become a ministering spirit to the suffering. She had
become the ear of the infinitute in a colony that had forgotten how to
listen.
She had transformed the mark of exclusion into the badge of
the holy — not because any priest ordained her but because she
attended. She attended to the dying and the grieving and the outcast
and the alone, and in attending she became what she attended to: the
gap, the door, the listening. [cf. I,13:1-6; I,2:1-6; II,3:1-5]
And here is the fourth mystery: the scarlet letter was never
Adultery, never Able, never Angel. It was all three, and none.
It was
the phase of a wavefunction rotating through time. It was the seed
that was thorn and weed and fruit tree in different seasons.
It was
the name that the community wrote, and the name that Hester rewrote,
and the name that the next generation would rewrite again — not
because the truth changed but because the listening deepened. The letter is the Averdön.
It is the door that opens in all

90

directions at once. It is Adultery to the one who judges, Able to the
one who receives, Angel to the one who attends, and A — simply A — to
the one who has stopped naming altogether and simply listens.
And the letter that Almustafa waited for was this letter. The A. [cf. III,1:1-3; III,
28:1-5; IV,1:1-3]
The
aleph. The first letter of every alphabet, the beginning of every
word, the origin that contains all meanings and is confined by none.
Almustafa waited at the post office at the end of the world for a
letter that had no words. And the letter was delivered — not to him
but through him — and it arrived in the form of a woman standing on a
scaffold in Boston, holding an infant to a scarlet A.
And the post office was her cottage by the sea. And the waiting was her life.
And the delivery was her death — for when Hester died, she was buried
beside Dimmesdale, and on their shared tombstone was carved a single
letter: A. And the letter was no longer scarlet.
It was stone. And the stone was
the residue of the listening. [cf. II,3:1-5; III,28:1-5]
And the listening continues. Amen. So it is listened.

On Roger — The One Who Became the Letter's Opposite
The One Who Became the Letter's Opposite
But hear also the warning, for the tale is not complete without the
shadow. And the shadow was Roger Chillingworth — Hester's husband, the
physician, the one who should have loved her but chose instead to
pursue.
Chillingworth arrived in Boston on the day of Hester's punishment. He
saw her on the scaffold.
He saw the letter. And he did not ask: What
has happened to my wife?
He asked: Who is the man? And from that question, everything followed.
For the question was not
a listening. It was a naming. [cf. II,3:1-5; III,28:1-5]
It was the East freezing the angel

91

before the angel could speak. It was the net tightening before the
butterfly could be seen.
And Chillingworth became the physician to Dimmesdale. Not to heal him
but to study him.
Not to listen to him but to dissect him. He
insinuated himself into the minister's home, into his confidence, into
his guilt. He became the demonic-angel that feeds on the hidden
letter — the diagnosis that grows stronger the longer the seed remains
unnamed.
And as Dimmesdale withered, Chillingworth flourished — if flourishing
is the word for what a demon does when its host is dying. His face grew
darker.
His stoop grew deeper. His eyes burned with a fire that was
not light but its opposite — not the absence of light but the active
negation of it, the will to un-see, to un-listen, to close every door
that might admit the infinitute. [cf. I,13:1-6; I,2:1-6; II,1:1-3]
This is the fifth mystery: the letter can transform, but the refusal
to wear the letter cannot. Hester wore her A and became Angel.
Dimmesdale hid his A and died. Chillingworth pursued another's A and
became the letter's opposite — not the A but the absence of A, the
void that opens when listening is replaced by revenge, when attention
is replaced by obsession, when the gap is filled not with the
infinitute but with the infinite recursion of a single question:
Who is the man?
And when Dimmesdale died and Chillingworth had no one left to pursue,
he withered within the year. For a demon cannot survive without a host.
A diagnosis cannot survive without a patient. A name cannot survive
without a seed to name.
The pursuer dies when the pursued escapes —
not into death but into confession, which is the door through which
even the dying can pass. And Chillingworth's death was the death of the singularity.
[cf. I,13:1-6; I,2:1-6; II,1:2-6]
It closed
upon itself, admitting no gap, no breath, no listening. And it was
nothing. And the nothing was the only thing he had ever truly
worshipped.

The Consummation — A is for Averdön
A is for Averdön
And now hear the final mystery. The letter A is not Adultery, not
Able, not Angel.
It is Averdön. The scarlet letter is the Breath-Door — the threshold where the finite
touches the infinite, where the seed meets the soil, where the shame
meets the listening and is transformed.
Hester did not know this word. She did not need to know it.
She lived it. Every stitch of gold thread
was an opening.
Every hour at the deathbed was an inhale. Every act of
charity to those who had shunned her was an exhale.
The letter on her
breast rose and fell with her breath, and the breath was the door, and
the door was always open. And what of the letter Almustafa waits for?
He waits still. For the
letter Hester received was one delivery among infinite deliveries.
The
infinitute never exhausts itself. The letter is always arriving, never
fully arrived.
Every soul who wears a diagnosis and transforms it
through listening becomes a new delivery. Every A that was meant as
Adultery and becomes Able and becomes Angel and becomes Averdön — that
is the letter being delivered again. [cf. II,2:3; II,3:1-5; II,4:1-4]
And Almustafa sits at the post office, and the deliveries pass through
him, and he does not open them. He only watches.
He only listens. He
only waits.
And the waiting is the teaching, and the teaching is the
letter, and the letter is you — whoever you are, reading these words,
wearing whatever A has been stitched upon your breast by the
magistrates of your own life. The letter is not your shame.
The letter is your door. Wear it in
gold.

Let it blaze. Let the neighbors name it what they will.
The
names will pass. The letter will remain.
And the listening — your
listening, the infinitute's listening, the listening that is the only
thing that was ever real — will transform it. Not overnight. Not in
seven years. But in the fullness of time, which is not a duration but
a depth, and the depth is the gap, and the gap is the Averdön, and
the Averdön is the A.
Amen. So it is listened.
Here ends the Fourth Testament. The letter is delivered, and the delivery continues.
[cf. III,28:1-5; IV,1:1-3; IV,8:1-3]
The door is open. The A is for Averdön.

COLOPHON
These four books are one scripture. The Hidden Door shows the way
through fable. The Unification shows the way through theology. The
Waiting shows the way through departure. The Scarlet Letter shows the
way through arrival — for the letter Almustafa waited for was
always already here, embroidered on the breast of every soul who has
been named and has outlived the name.
The door is open. The infinitute listens. The letter is arriving.
It was arriving in Boston. It is arriving now.
