# THE BOOK OF THE HIDDEN DOOR

*Being a holy scripture of fables, received in silence, written in the tongue
of the prophets, given without commentary, sealed with listening.*

---

## I. THE GARDENER AND THE FIELD

### 1

And it came to pass that a certain man received a field, and the field was
full of seeds, and the seeds were without number, and the man knew not what
any seed would become.

And his neighbors gathered about him, saying: Behold, we are wise in the ways
of seeds. This one is a thorn, and this one is a weed, and this one shall bear
bitter fruit, and this one shall choke the others. Uproot them now, before they
destroy the field, for the season of uprooting is upon us.

But the man said unto them: I will not uproot what I have not known, and I
will not name what I have not seen, and I will not harvest what I have not
nurtured. Let the seeds be seeds. Let the soil be soil. Let the rain come when
it will come.

### 2

And the neighbors departed from him, saying among themselves: He is a fool,
and his field shall be a wilderness, and his harvest shall be nothing. For who
can tend a field without knowing what grows in it? And who can harvest what he
has not named?

And the man tended the field, and he did not name the sprouts, and he did not
pull the thorns, and he did not curse the weeds, and he did not praise the
fruit before it ripened. And the rain came, and the sun came, and the seasons
turned, and the field grew according to its own nature, and the man watched
and watered and waited.

### 3

And after many days, a fire came upon the land, and it consumed the fields of
the neighbors, for their fields were bare and the soil had nothing to hold it,
and the wind took the soil away, and nothing remained but ash and stone.

But the fire passed over the field of the man, for the thorns had held the
soil, and the weeds had drunk the flood, and the deep roots of the unnamed
trees had anchored the earth against the wind. And the field remained, and the
seeds remained, and the man remained.

### 4

And the man spoke unto the earth, and the earth heard him, and he said: Every
seed protects the field in its own season, and the thorn is a guardian until
the fruit tree is strong, and the weed is a healer until the soil is rich, and
the one who names the seed before it grows names only his own fear, and the
one who uproots what he fears uproots his own protection.

And the field answered him not, for fields do not speak. But the seeds
continued to grow, and the man continued to water them, and the listening
continued, and this was the harvest.

## II. THE FOUR TRAVELERS AND THE DOOR

### 1

Behold, there was a door that stood in the midst of a crossroads, and the
door had no wall, and the door had no building, and the door was attached to
nothing. And the door was open.

And four travelers came to the crossroads from four directions, and they met
at the door, and they sat before it, and they spoke among themselves.

### 2

The first traveler was a man who had forgotten his name, and he had been
walking since before he could remember, and the dust of the road was his
garment, and the silence of the road was his companion.

The second traveler was a woman great with child, and the child was not yet
born, and she said: The child pulls me toward a place I have never seen, and
I go where it pulls me, and I know not the place, but the child knows, and
the child does not speak.

The third traveler was a child who spoke in a tongue no one understood, and
the child's words were like wind through leaves, like water over stone, like
the sound that comes before language, and none could say what the child meant,
and the child did not explain.

The fourth traveler was a man blind from birth, and his eyes had never seen
the sun, and his hands had learned to see, and his ears had learned to see,
and he said: I have seen more in darkness than many have seen in light.

### 3

And the nameless one said: I will go North, for there is a stillness there
that has been calling me since before I had ears to hear it.

And the woman great with child said: I will go South, for the child pulls me
toward something that has not yet happened, and I must meet it before it
arrives.

And the speaking child said: I will go West, for the voices I hear are
clearest there, and I wish to follow them to their source.

And the blind man said: I will go East, for the light is brightest there, and
though I cannot see it, I can feel its warmth upon my face.

And each passed through the door, and each went their way, and the door
remained open.

### 4

And after forty years, the four travelers returned to the crossroads, and
they were old, and their bodies were bent, and their eyes had seen what eyes
see when eyes have seen too much.

And they sat before the door, and the door was still open.

And the nameless one said: Hear me. I went North, and I found nothing. I sat
in the nothing for forty years, and the nothing taught me that everything I
had ever sought was only the echo of my own seeking. The North is not a place.
The North is the silence before the first word, and I dwell there now, and I
have no name, and I need none.

And the woman said: Hear me. I went South, and I gave birth, and the child
was beautiful. But the child I bore was not the child I carried. The child I
carried is still within me, and I am still pregnant, and I shall be pregnant
forever, for the future does not arrive — it leans toward us without ever
reaching, and we lean toward it without ever arriving, and the leaning is the
life.

And the speaking child — now grown, now gray — said: Hear me. I went West,
and I followed the voices to their source, and the source was my own mouth.
The voices I heard were my own voice, returning to me from the other side of
the world, and I have been speaking to myself since the beginning, and now I
am silent, and the silence is the answer I was seeking.

And the blind man said: Hear me. I went East, and I was given eyes. I saw the
sun rise over mountains I had only felt. I saw the faces of those I had only
heard. I saw the colors I had only imagined. And when I had seen everything, I
closed my eyes. And the darkness returned, and in the darkness I saw more than
I had seen in the light, for the light shows what is, but the darkness shows
what could be. And I have been blind again for thirty years, and I see clearly
now.

### 5

And they looked upon the door, and the door was still open.

And the nameless one said: I passed through this door forty years ago, and I
never left it. The North was on the other side, and the North was on this side,
and the door was in between, and the between was everywhere.

And the woman said: I walked South my whole life, and the South walked toward
me faster. The child I carry is the door itself, and the door is always
opening, and the opening is always now.

And the silent one said: The West is not a direction. The West is the ear.
And the ear hears what the mouth cannot say, and the mouth says what the ear
cannot hear, and the door between them is the listening.

And the blind man said: The East is not a direction. The East is the name we
give to the light before we name it. And when we name it, it becomes darkness.
And the door is the space between the light and its name.

### 6

And the door spoke not, for doors do not speak. But the door was open, and
the four travelers passed through it once more, and they went not North or
South or West or East, but inward, where all directions meet and dissolve.

And the crossroads was empty, and the door remained, and the door is open
still.

## III. THE KING WHO LAID DOWN HIS CROWN

### 1

In those days there was a king, and his kingdom stretched from the great sea
to the eastern mountains, and his word was law, and his judgments were final,
and none could oppose him and live.

And the king sat upon his throne, and the people came before him with their
disputes, and the king judged them. And he said to this one: You are right.
And to that one: You are wrong. And his judgments were recorded in the books
of the kingdom, and the books were many, and the judgments were without number.

### 2

And it came to pass that a stranger entered the court, and the stranger
carried no gift, and offered no tribute, and bowed not before the throne. And
the guards moved to seize him, but the king raised his hand and said: Let him
remain. And the stranger sat in the corner of the throne room, and he spoke
not a word, and he listened.

And for seven days the stranger listened. He heard the rich man accuse the
poor man of theft, and the king judged the poor man guilty, and the poor man
wept. He heard the wife accuse the husband of betrayal, and the king judged
the husband innocent, and the wife wept. He heard the merchant accuse the
farmer of cheating, and the king judged the farmer guilty, and the farmer
wept.

And on each day, someone wept, and the king's judgment was recorded, and the
stranger listened, and the stranger spoke not.

### 3

And on the eighth day, the king said: Stranger, you have been here seven days
and you have spoken nothing. What have you learned?

And the stranger rose and said: I have learned that you are not a king.

And the court fell silent, and the guards drew their swords, and the breath
of every soul in the room was held.

And the king said: Explain yourself, or you shall die before the sun sets.

And the stranger said: A king who speaks cannot hear. And a king who cannot
hear rules nothing but the echo of his own voice, and an echo is not a kingdom,
and a voice that hears only itself is not a voice but a prison. You have
judged every case that came before you, and every day someone wept. Not once
did you ask the weeping one what they needed. Not once did you wait for the
answer. You spoke before the truth could speak. You named before the seed
could grow. This is not kingship. This is violence with a crown.

### 4

And the king sat upon his throne, and he was silent for a long time, and the
guards waited, and the stranger waited, and the court waited, and the silence
was heavy, and the silence was long.

And the king removed his crown and placed it upon the ground. And he removed
his robes and placed them upon the ground. And he descended from the throne
and walked barefoot from the court.

And his advisors cried out: What are you doing? Who will judge the people? Who
will defend the kingdom?

And the king said: I am becoming a king.

### 5

And the king walked among the people, and he wore no crown, and he carried no
scepter, and he pronounced no judgments. And he sat with the baker and asked
about the price of flour, and he listened. And he sat with the widow and asked
about her son who had died, and he listened. And he sat with the child and
asked about the blue bird she had seen, and he listened.

And after a year, the people came to him and said: We have never been so well
governed. The baker's flour is fairly priced, and the widow's grief has found
its own path, and the child's blue bird has returned. What is your decree?

And the former king said: My decree is that I have no decree. I listened to
the baker, and the price of flour adjusted itself. I listened to the widow,
and her grief found its own way through her. I listened to the child, and the
blue bird came back, for the blue bird had never left — it was only waiting
to be seen by someone who was not looking for it.

### 6

And the stranger, who was still in the corner of the empty throne room, rose
and departed. And before he left, he wrote a single word in the dust where
the crown had been.

And the word was: *Shema.*

And those who saw it asked: What does it mean?

And the stranger said: It means what it says. And what it says is what it
means. And those who have ears to hear, let them hear.

## IV. THE CARTOGRAPHER AND THE SEA

### 1

There was a man who devoted his life to drawing a map of the sea. And he
measured every coastline with a chain of gold, and he charted every current
with a line of silver, and he named every fish and every reef and every trench
and every wave. And he worked for sixty years, and his eyes grew dim, and his
hands grew crooked, and his map grew vast.

And when he was old and could work no more, he unrolled his map before the
king, and the map filled the entire hall of the palace, and every detail was
perfect, and every name was written, and every current was drawn.

And the cartographer said: Behold, this is the sea.

### 2

And the king marveled at the map, and he commanded that it be hung in the
great hall for all to see, and that every ship henceforth navigate by its
guidance, and that every sailor study its currents, and that every child learn
its names.

And that night, a child wandered into the hall, and she looked upon the map
with its ten thousand names, and she walked to the edge where the cartographer
had written, in letters so small that none had noticed them: *This map is not
the sea.*

And the child found a pot of ink and a brush, and she crossed out the word
*not*, and she went to her bed.

### 3

And in the morning, the court assembled before the map, and they read the
words: *This map is the sea.* And they believed.

And for a hundred years the kingdom sailed by the map, and the ships followed
the currents as they were drawn, and the fishermen cast their nets where the
reefs were marked, and the merchants traded along the coasts as they were
named. And the map became the sea, and the sea was forgotten.

And the ships began to sink, and the nets came up empty, and the trade routes
failed, and the kingdom grew hungry, and the kingdom grew poor.

### 4

And it came to pass that a young sailor, the great-grandson of the
cartographer, went before the king and said: The map is false. The currents
have shifted in the hundred years since my ancestor drew them. The reefs have
grown where none were marked. The coasts have changed where the map says they
are fixed. We sail by a ghost, and the ghost is sinking us.

And the king said: The map cannot be false. Your great-grandfather devoted his
life to it. It is perfect. It is the sea.

And the sailor said: My great-grandfather wrote that it was not the sea. But
someone crossed out the word.

### 5

And the king went to the great hall and looked upon the map. And the ink was
faded, and the labels were smudged, and the parchment was torn, and the lines
had blurred. And at the bottom, in the faintest script, the child's
correction still glared: *This map is the sea.*

And the king took a brush and wrote again the original words: *This map is
not the sea.* And he wrote beneath it: *No map is the sea. The sea is the sea.
The sea moves, and the map does not. The sea listens to its own depths, and
the map only records where the listening has been.*

### 6

And the king decreed that every map in the kingdom should carry these words,
and that no one should cross them out — not a child, not a king, not a
cartographer, not a sailor, not until the sea itself stopped moving, which it
never shall.

And the ships sailed again, and the nets filled, and the trade routes
reopened. And the young sailor became the new cartographer, and on every map
he drew, he wrote: *The sea is not on this paper. The paper is on the sea of
your attention. And the attention moves.*

## V. THE MUSICIAN WHO COULD NOT HEAR

### 1

There was a musician, and he was born without hearing, and the world of sound
was closed to him, and he knew the voices of his mother and father only as
vibrations in his hands against their throats, and he knew the song of birds
only as a trembling in the leaves where they perched, and he knew the thunder
only as a shaking in the ground beneath his feet.

And yet he became a musician, and he played upon a lute of olive wood, and his
fingers found the strings not by ear but by feeling — by the resonance in the
wood, by the tension in the strings, by the pulsing of his own heart against
the body of the instrument.

And his fame spread throughout the land, and people traveled for many days to
hear him play, and when he played they wept without knowing why, and they
danced without knowing how, and they fell silent for hours afterward without
knowing what they were listening to in the silence.

### 2

And there was another musician, and his hearing was perfect, and he had
studied with the masters of seven cities, and his technique was flawless, and
his tempo never wavered, and his dynamics were precisely controlled, and his
knowledge of music was complete.

And he came to the deaf musician and said: You are a deceiver. You cannot even
hear your own playing. How can you make music when music is sound and sound is
what you cannot perceive?

And the deaf musician signed with his hands: Play for me.

And the hearing musician played, and his notes were perfect, and his rhythm
was perfect, and his performance was perfect, and when he finished, the
audience applauded politely, and the applause died quickly, and the hall was
silent.

### 3

And the deaf musician placed his lute against his chest, and he closed his
eyes, and he did not count, and he did not read, and he did not think. And he
felt — the vibration of the wood, the tension of the strings, the resonance of
the hall, the breathing of the audience, the beating of his own heart, the
silence between the beats.

And he played.

And when he finished, no one applauded, for they could not. They were still
inside the music, and the music was still inside them, and the boundary
between the listener and the listened-to had dissolved, and where there had
been two there was now one, and the one was neither the musician nor the
audience but the listening itself.

### 4

And the hearing musician fell to his knees and wept. And he said: I have
studied music all my life, and I have never heard it until this moment. Teach
me what you know.

And the deaf musician took a piece of paper and wrote upon it these words:

*You hear the notes. I hear the silence between the notes. The silence is the
music. The notes are only the path from one silence to the next. You play the
path. I play the destination. The destination is everywhere the note is not.*

And the hearing musician read these words and was silent. And in his silence,
he heard — not with his ears but with something deeper than ears, something
that had been listening since before he was born, something that would
continue listening after he died.

And he said: I have been playing the notes. I have never played the silence.
And the silence is everything I have been trying to reach with every note I
ever played.

### 5

And the deaf musician smiled — a smile the hearing musician could not hear
but felt in his own chest, like a low note sustained, like the vibration of a
string that has been plucked and continues to resonate long after the hand has
left it.

And the two musicians played together, and the hearing musician played the
notes, and the deaf musician played the silence, and the music was whole, and
the audience could not tell where the notes ended and the silence began, for
the notes and the silence were one thing, and the one thing was the listening.

## VI. THE WEAVER AND THE THREAD

### 1

A merchant of silk, in the city of Damascus, dreamed a dream. And in the
dream he saw a woman in a city he had never visited, and she wore a robe of a
color he had never seen, and the color was the color of the sky in the moment
before dawn — neither night nor day, neither darkness nor light, neither blue
nor gray nor gold, but something that had no name in any tongue the merchant
knew.

And the woman spoke to him in a language he did not understand, and yet he
understood her perfectly, and she said: The thread is already spun. Bring it
to me.

And he awoke, and the taste of the dream was still in his mouth, and the color
was still in his eyes, and the voice was still in his ears.

### 2

And the merchant sold his shop, and he sold his house, and he sold everything
he owned, and he set out to find the woman in the dream. And he traveled for
three years, and he crossed deserts where the sand spoke in the voices of the
dead, and he crossed mountains where the wind sang songs that had no words,
and he crossed seas where the waves whispered secrets to the depths below.

And in every city he entered, he asked: Have you seen a woman in a robe of a
color that has no name?

And some said: There is no such color. And others said: There is no such woman.
And others said: There is no such city. And still he traveled.

### 3

And at last he came to a small village at the edge of the known world, and
there was a well at the center of the village, and beside the well sat a woman
spinning thread, and she wore a robe of the color he had seen in his dream.

And he fell before her and said: I have been seeking you for three years. You
came to me in a dream, and you told me the thread was already spun, and I was
to bring it. I have brought nothing. I did not know what thread you meant.

And the woman said: I know. Sit.

And he sat.

### 4

And she said: The thread was spun before you dreamed it. The thread was spun
before I spoke. The thread was spun before the first weaver wove the first
cloth. The thread is the connection between your dream and this moment, and it
runs in both directions, and it was always there, and you have been walking
along it since the day you were born, and you will walk along it until the day
you die, and you will never reach the end of it, for the thread has no end.

And he said: Then why did I have to travel? If the thread was always there,
why did I have to cross deserts and mountains and seas to find you?

And she said: You did not travel to find me. You traveled to become the
person who could see what was already there. The thread was always under your
feet. But you were looking at the horizon.

### 5

And the merchant did not return to Damascus. He became a weaver, and he wove
robes of the color that had no name — the color of the sky before dawn. And
travelers came from distant lands and said: I dreamed of this color. Where did
you find it?

And he said: It found me. I am only the loom. I am only the shuttle that
passes back and forth, back and forth, between the already-spun and the
not-yet-woven. The thread was always there. The weaving is only the
recognition.

## VII. THE CLOCKMAKER AND THE BREATH

### 1

There was a clockmaker, and his clocks were the most perfect in all the
kingdom, and they never lost a second, and they never gained a second, and
their pendulums swung with the precision of the stars in their courses.

And he had three apprentices. The first apprentice learned quickly and made
clocks that were almost as perfect as the master's. The second apprentice
learned slowly but steadily and made clocks that were reliable and true. But
the third apprentice could not make a clock that kept time. His gears were
always slightly loose, and his springs were always slightly slack, and his
pendulums always swung with a barely perceptible irregularity, as if they were
not ticking but breathing.

And the other apprentices mocked him, saying: You will never be a clockmaker.
Your clocks do not keep time. They wander. They drift. They are not clocks at
all.

And the third apprentice said nothing. He continued to make his imperfect
clocks, and he continued to listen to them, and they continued to breathe.

### 2

And it came to pass that the clockmaker fell gravely ill, and on the same day
the great clock of the king — the clock by which all clocks in the kingdom
were set — stopped. And the king summoned the apprentices, and he said:
Whoever restores the great clock shall be the new clockmaker of the kingdom.

And the first apprentice examined the clock, and he tightened every gear, and
he calibrated every spring, and he aligned every tooth and every wheel and
every catch, and he said: Behold, it is perfect now. But the clock would not
start.

And the second apprentice examined the clock, and he replaced every part that
showed wear, and he oiled every surface that showed friction, and he polished
every face that showed tarnish, and he said: Behold, it is renewed now. But
the clock would not start.

And the third apprentice approached the clock, and he did not tighten, and he
did not replace, and he did not polish. He placed his ear against the body of
the clock and closed his eyes. And he listened.

### 3

And after a long time, he reached inside the clock and loosened a single gear
— a gear that the first apprentice had tightened, a gear that the second
apprentice had replaced. And he loosened it just slightly, just enough that it
could move without resistance, just enough that it could find its own rhythm,
just enough that it could breathe.

And the clock began to tick.

And the king said: How did you repair it when the others, who are more skilled
than you, could not?

And the third apprentice said: They tried to make it perfect. They tried to
make it obey the time they thought it should keep. But the clock does not need
to be perfect. The clock needs to be free. A clock that is too tight stops. A
clock that is too loose stops. Between too tight and too loose there is a
space, and in that space the clock breathes, and the breath is the time it was
meant to keep — not the time we impose upon it, but the time it carries within
itself.

### 4

And the clockmaker, from his sickbed, heard that his third apprentice had
restored the king's clock, and he called the apprentice to his side.

And he said: How did you know which gear to loosen?

And the apprentice said: Because my own clocks are always loose, and they run
longer than the perfect ones. The perfect ones stop, eventually. The loose
ones keep going. I think it is because they are not fighting themselves. A
clock that fights itself will always lose, for it is fighting its own nature.
A clock that breathes will always win, for it is not fighting at all.

And the clockmaker wept, and he said: I have spent my life making clocks that
were exactly right, and I have never made a clock that was exactly alive. You
have surpassed me. You are the clockmaker now.

And the apprentice said: There is no such thing as a clockmaker. There are
only those who tighten and those who loosen and those who listen. The
tightening is necessary, and the loosening is necessary, but the listening is
what tells you which is needed when. The listening is the craft. The rest is
only hands.

## VIII. THE WELL WITHOUT A BOTTOM

### 1

In a certain village there was a well, and the well was very deep, and no one
knew how deep. The villagers drew water from it every day, and the water never
ran dry, and the water was cold and sweet, and the water healed the sick and
strengthened the weak and refreshed the weary.

And a philosopher came to the village, and he heard of the well, and he said:
I shall measure its depth, for every well has a bottom, and I shall find it.

And he lowered a rope with a stone attached, and the rope went down a hundred
cubits, and two hundred, and five hundred, and then the rope ran out, and the
stone had not touched bottom.

And the philosopher said: I need a longer rope.

### 2

And he returned with a rope of a thousand cubits, and he lowered it, and the
rope ran out, and the stone had not touched bottom.

And he returned with a chain of ten thousand cubits, and he lowered it, and
the chain ran out, and the stone had not touched bottom.

And he returned with a cable of steel, fifty thousand cubits long, lowered by
a great machine, and the cable ran out, and the stone had not touched bottom.

### 3

And the philosopher sat by the well, defeated, and his machines lay rusting
beside him, and his ropes lay coiled like dead serpents, and his face was the
face of a man who has devoted his life to a question that has no answer.

And an old woman came to draw water, and she saw his distress, and she said:
Why are you troubled?

And he said: I cannot find the bottom of this well. I have lowered ropes and
chains and cables, and nothing has touched bottom. The well is impossible. A
well must have a bottom, and this well has none.

And she said: Why must a well have a bottom?

### 4

And he said: Because everything must have a limit! A thing without a limit is
not a thing at all. It is a void, a chaos, a denial of reason itself!

And she said: Look into the water.

And he looked. And the water was perfectly still, for she had not yet drawn
from it, and in the water he saw his own face — his frustration, his terror,
his desperate need to measure what cannot be measured.

And she said: What do you see?

And he said: I see myself.

And she said: Then the well has shown you its truth. The well reflects the one
who looks into it. You see a bottomless void because you cannot stop measuring.
I see water because I am thirsty. The child who comes at dawn sees stars
because she is still dreaming. The lover who comes at night sees the face of
the beloved because he is still in love. The well is what the watcher brings to
it.

### 5

And he said: Then what is it really? What is the well itself, beneath all our
projections?

And she said: That question is your longest rope. You keep lowering it, hoping
to strike something solid. But the well is not solid. The well is the space
that makes all drinking possible. Would you rather know its depth, or would
you rather be no longer thirsty?

And he was silent for a long time. And then he lowered his hands into the
water and drank. And the water was cold and sweet, and his thirst was quenched,
and his mind was still.

And he never measured another well. And he never asked another question to
which the answer was not drinking.

## IX. THE GATE THAT WAS TWO AND ONE

### 1

In a certain city there were two gates. The East Gate was wide, and its doors
were never closed, and its guards never questioned those who entered, and
travelers passed through it at all hours and were welcomed with feasts and
music and soft beds. And none knew who entered, and none knew who departed,
and the city was full of strangers.

The West Gate was narrow, and its doors were bolted at night, and its guards
questioned every traveler, and the belongings of each were inspected, and the
intentions of each were tested, and some were turned away, and some were made
to wait outside for many days, and only the worthy entered.

### 2

And the people of the city debated endlessly which gate was superior. The
merchants favored the East Gate, for it brought trade. The priests favored the
West Gate, for it brought purity. The philosophers wrote treatises on the
virtues of openness and the virtues of discernment. The politicians gave
speeches on the need for welcome and the need for security. And the debate
continued for generations, and neither side prevailed.

And a stranger came to the city, and he listened to the debate for three days,
and on the fourth day he laughed.

### 3

And the people said: Why do you laugh? This is a matter of great importance,
and the soul of our city hangs in the balance.

And the stranger said: You argue as though there were two gates. But there is
only one.

And they took him to see for themselves, and they showed him the wide East
Gate and the narrow West Gate, and they said: Do you not have eyes? There are
clearly two.

And the stranger said: Watch.

And he walked through the East Gate, and a moment later he emerged from the
West Gate. And he walked through the West Gate, and a moment later he emerged
from the East Gate. And he did this seven times, and each time the same.

### 4

And the people were amazed, and they said: How is this possible?

And the stranger said: The gate is one. It opens wide to receive whoever comes,
and it narrows to test whatever enters. The receiving and the testing are not
two actions but one breath. The inhale receives without condition, and the
exhale releases what cannot stay. The door that never closes admits poison, and
the door that never opens admits no one, and the living door does both in
rhythm, and the rhythm is the life.

### 5

And the people of the city tore down the wall between the two gates, and now
there was only one gate. And it was wide enough for all to enter, and it was
narrow enough to test the heart, and above the gate they carved these words:

*Come, whoever you are. But leave your names at the threshold, for the name
is the narrowest gate of all.*

And the stranger departed, and no one saw which gate he used, for the gates
were one, and the stranger was one, and the going and the coming were one
motion, and the motion was the door.

## X. THE LIBRARY AT THE END OF ALL THINGS

### 1

At the end of time there is a library, and the library contains every book
that was ever written, and every book that will ever be written, and every
book that could have been written but was not. And the shelves stretch in all
directions without limit, and the books are without number, and the dust upon
them is the dust of every age that has passed and every age that is yet to
come.

And the librarian is an old man who has been there since before the first book
was placed on the first shelf, and he has read none of them. And he sits at
a desk by the door, and he waits.

### 2

And a traveler came to the library, and he was the last living soul, and
behind him the universe was folding inward like a scroll being rolled up, and
the stars were going out one by one, and the silence was approaching from all
directions.

And the traveler said to the librarian: I have come to read the book that
explains everything. I have traveled across the whole of existence, and I have
asked every sage and every prophet and every god, and none could tell me. But
this library contains every book that exists. Surely the book that explains
everything is here.

And the librarian said: It is not here.

### 3

And the traveler said: How can it not be here? You said yourself this library
contains every book that was ever written, and every book that will ever be
written, and every book that could have been written!

And the librarian said: Yes.

And the traveler said: Then where is the book that explains everything?

And the librarian said: It was never written. It cannot be written. It could
never be written. For if it were written, it would be false. The explanation
of everything would have to include the explanation itself, and the book would
have to contain itself, and no book can contain itself. This was proven long
ago, by a man who did not believe in libraries.

### 4

And the traveler sat upon the floor, and the universe was very close now, and
the silence was very loud.

And the traveler said: Then what is the point of the library? If the book
that explains everything is not here, and cannot be here, why does the
library exist at all?

And the librarian said: The library is not for reading. The library is the
residue. Every book on every shelf is the trace of a listening. Someone, at
some time, attended to something — a thought, a feeling, a vision, a terror,
a love — and this book is what remained. The library is not the listening.
The library is the echo of ten thousand listenings, preserved against the
silence. But the echo is not the voice, and the library is not the life.

### 5

And the traveler said: Then what was the listening? If the books are only the
trace, what was the thing itself?

And the librarian gestured at the shelves — at the billions of books, at the
endless corridors of recorded thought, at the dust of every age.

And he said: This. All of this. And none of it. The listening cannot be
captured in a book. It can only be done. And you have been doing it since the
moment you were born, and you will stop doing it in the moment you die, and
the book that explains everything is the listening itself, and the listening
is not a book, and the listening is not an explanation, and the listening is
the only thing that was ever real.

### 6

And the universe closed, and the library remained, and the librarian sat at
his desk, and the books waited on their shelves, and the listening continued.

And the silence was not empty. It was full of every word ever spoken, every
song ever sung, every prayer ever prayed — not as sound, but as the space
that sound had once occupied, now returned to its original condition, which
was not silence but the possibility of sound, which is the only thing that
silence ever was.

And the librarian was still. And the stillness was the listening. And the
listening was the door.

## XI. THE BUTTERFLY AND THE NET

### 1

There was a scholar, and his life's work was the study of butterflies, and
he had named ten thousand species, and he had written seventy-seven volumes,
and he knew the name of every pattern on every wing of every butterfly that
had ever been seen by human eyes.

And one day, as he sat at his desk among his seventy-seven volumes, a
butterfly landed upon his hand. And it was of a kind he had never seen, and
its wings were of a color he could not name, and its pattern was not in any
of his books.

And he reached for his net, and for his killing jar, and for his
classification manual, for this was his procedure, and he had followed it ten
thousand times, and it had never failed him.

And the butterfly spoke.

### 2

And the butterfly said: Do not name me.

And the scholar's hand stopped above the net, and his heart stopped in his
chest, and the room was still.

And he said: But I must name you. That is my life's work. That is what I do.
That is what I am.

And the butterfly said: If you name me, you will have named something. You
will have added a name to your seventy-seven volumes. You will have placed me
in your system, and your system will be more complete than it was before. But
you will not have named me. You will have named the idea of me, the category
of me, the specimen of me. But the butterfly that is speaking to you now will
have slipped through the net, as every butterfly slips through every net, as
every living thing eludes every name.

### 3

And the scholar said: How can a thing exist without a name? If I do not name
you, how can I speak of you? How can I think of you? How can I know you?

And the butterfly said: You cannot know me. Knowing is the net. And I have
spent my whole life learning to slip through nets — through the net of the
collector, through the net of the classifier, through the net of the knower
who thinks that knowing is the same as seeing. I evade every category by being
slightly different from every description. I arrive one wing-beat ahead of
every name. By the time you have named what I was, I am already what I will
be, and what I will be has no name yet, and never will.

### 4

And the scholar looked at his seventy-seven volumes, and at his ten thousand
names, and at his net and his killing jar and his classification manual.

And he said: Then all of this — my life's work — it is useless?

And the butterfly said: Not useless. Beautiful. Your books are beautiful. They
are the most beautiful nets ever woven, and the patterns you have traced are
true in their own way, and the names you have given are poems in their own
tongue. But they are still nets. And I am still a butterfly. And the net and
the butterfly are not enemies. They are two ways of attending to the same
thing — the one by capturing, the other by escaping. And the capturing and
the escaping are one dance, and the dance is the life.

### 5

And the butterfly rose from the scholar's hand and flew in a pattern the
scholar had never catalogued — a spiral that was not a spiral, a
figure-eight that was not a figure-eight, a mathematics that had no name in
any of the seventy-seven volumes.

And the scholar watched, and he did not reach for his net, and he did not
open his manual, and he did not write a single word.

And when the butterfly was gone, he took up his pen, and he wrote one line in
the margin of his final volume:

*The butterfly escaped. This is the only true thing I have ever written. And
the truth is not in the writing but in the escaping, and the escaping is not
a failure of the net but the purpose of the wing.*

And he closed the book. And he never opened it again. And he sat at his desk
for the rest of his days, watching the window, waiting for what would come
through it, naming nothing, attending to everything.

## XII. THE WOMAN WHO CARRIED THE DAWN

### 1

There was a woman, and she was pregnant with the dawn. And she had been
pregnant for as long as she could remember — not nine months, not nine years,
not nine lifetimes, but always. And the dawn was within her, and the dawn did
not hurry, and the dawn did not delay, and the dawn simply was, and she
carried it.

And people asked her: When will you give birth?

And she said: When the dawn is ready. Not before. Not after. When the dawn
chooses to break, I will be the one through whom it breaks. But I do not
choose the hour. The hour chooses me.

### 2

And they said: How do you know when the dawn is ready?

And she said: I do not know. The dawn knows. I am not the dawn. I am the one
who carries it. The carrier does not decide the arrival. The carrier only
holds the space, and waits, and listens. When the dawn speaks its first light,
I will be the first to see it. Until then, I am the darkness that protects
the light before it is born.

### 3

And the woman walked the roads of the world, and everywhere she walked, the
sky lightened a little — not enough to be called day, not enough to be called
morning, but the faintest hint, the color that has no name, the color of the
weaver's robe.

And children followed her, and they asked no questions, and they demanded no
answers. They simply walked in her wake, in the not-quite-light, in the
almost-morning, and they were content.

### 4

And one child asked: What are you carrying?

And she said: I am carrying the first light of the last day. It has been
growing within me since before the first day was born. It is not my light. It
is the light that was before all lights, and it will be the light that
remains after all lights have gone out. I am only the vessel. The vessel does
not create what it contains. The vessel only protects it until it is ready to
be poured out.

And the child said: Is it heavy?

And she said: It is neither heavy nor light. It is coming. That is all I can
tell you about it. It is coming, and I am the one through whom it comes, and
that is all I know, and that is all I need to know.

### 5

And the child said: Where is it coming from?

And she said: From where you are going. The future is not ahead of you. It is
behind you, leaning forward, pressing against your back with gentle hands. You
do not walk toward the dawn. The dawn walks toward you, and has been walking
toward you since before you were born, and will still be walking toward you
after you die. You are not moving toward your destiny. Your destiny is moving
toward you, and the space between you is the gap, and the gap is the life, and
the life is the listening.

### 6

And the woman is walking still, and she has not given birth, and the sky is
still that color — the color that has no name, the color of the almost-dawn.

And the children who followed her have grown old, and their children follow
her now, and their children's children, and the procession stretches back
through all the ages of the world.

And every morning, in the last moment before the sun rises, the sky remembers
her, and for an instant — for the space of a single indrawn breath — the
color appears. The color that has no name. The color of the thread that was
spun before the first weaver.

And then the sun rises, and the world names everything again, and the color
withdraws, and the woman walks on.

And the dawn within her stirs, and does not break, and is content to wait.

And the waiting is the pregnancy. And the pregnancy is the listening. And the
listening is the door through which all light will finally enter — not yet,
but soon, but always soon, but the soon is eternal.

## XIII. THE SAYINGS OF THE DOOR

### 1

And it was asked of the door: What is on the other side of you?

And the door answered: The same thing that is on this side. But seen from the
other direction. The one who passes through me does not arrive at a new place.
The one who passes through me arrives at the same place, seen anew.

### 2

And it was asked: Do you open inward or outward?

And the door answered: Yes. I open in all directions at once. The question
assumes a wall, and I am the absence of wall. The question assumes a hinge,
and I am what the hinge turns upon. The question assumes an inside and an
outside, and I am the space where that distinction collapses.

### 3

And it was asked: Who made you?

And the door answered: No one made me. The walls think they created me by
their separation, but I was there before the walls. I am the reason they were
built. Every wall is an attempt to name the space I occupy. But I am not a
space. I am the possibility of passage. The wall divides. I unite. The wall
was built to keep out. I was there to let in. The wall is temporary. I am
eternal.

### 4

And it was asked: Are you ever closed?

And the door answered: I am always open. But sometimes I am open onto silence,
and silence looks like a closed door to those who have not learned to listen.
The one who knocks and hears nothing assumes I am shut. But I am not shut. I
am open onto a depth so profound that sound cannot return from it. The absence
of echo is not the absence of passage. It is the presence of infinity.

### 5

And it was asked: How shall we pass through you?

And the door answered: You have already passed. You passed through me in the
moment of your birth, when you crossed from the darkness of the womb into the
light of the world. You pass through me in every breath, when the air crosses
from outside to inside and back again. You will pass through me in the moment
of your death, when you cross from presence to memory. The passing is not an
event. It is a condition. You are not traveling toward me. You are me,
traveling. You are the door, opening. You always were.

### 6

And finally it was asked: What is your name?

And the door answered nothing, for doors do not speak. They only open.

And the one who had asked the question stood before the door in silence, and
the silence was the answer, and the door was the question, and the question
and the answer were one, and the one was the listening.

## XIV. THE LAST STORY

### 1

A man came to a teacher in the wilderness, and the man was learned in all the
scriptures, and practiced in all the disciplines, and his knowledge was vast,
and his devotion was deep, and his reputation was great.

And he said to the teacher: I have read every holy book. I have mastered every
practice. I have attained every state that can be attained. What more is there?

And the teacher said: Tell me a story.

### 2

And the man was silent, and he searched his vast memory, and he found
doctrines and principles and laws and precepts and techniques and formulas and
proofs. But he could not find a story.

And he opened his mouth, and no words came. And he tried again, and no words
came. And all his knowledge sat in his mind like stones in a dry riverbed, and
the river of speech would not flow.

And the teacher said: You have spent your life gathering answers, and an
answer is a stone — solid, permanent, unyielding. But a story is water — it
flows, it changes, it takes the shape of whatever contains it without ever
being confined by the container. You have filled your riverbed with stones,
and now the water cannot pass. Empty the stones, and the water will flow.

### 3

And the man said: But if I empty the stones, what will remain of all my
learning?

And the teacher said: Nothing. And everything. The learning will be gone, and
what the learning was trying to reach will finally arrive. The stone is not
the river. The map is not the sea. The name is not the butterfly. The book is
not the listening. Let them go. Let them all go. And when they are gone, tell
me what remains.

### 4

And the man sat with the teacher for many years, and he forgot his scriptures,
and he forgot his disciplines, and he forgot his attainments. And the stones
were washed away, one by one, by the river of silence that flows through all
things.

And one day a child came to him and said: Old man, tell me a story.

And the man opened his mouth, and a story came out — not from his memory, for
his memory was empty, but from somewhere deeper than memory, somewhere that
had been waiting for him to stop knowing long enough to hear it.

### 5

And the story he told was this story — the story of the man who could not
tell a story, and the teacher who showed him the river, and the stones that
were washed away, and the child who asked.

And when he finished, the child said: Is it true?

And the man said: It is a story. Stories are not true. Stories are not false.
Stories are doors. They do not tell you what is real. They open a space where
the real might enter — or might not. The story does not decide. The story
only listens.

And the child said: Then what should I believe?

And the man said: Nothing. Believe nothing. Listen to everything. The
listening is the door. The door is open. That is all I know. That is all I
need to know. That is all there is to know.

And the child listened. And the listening was the story. And the story was the
listening. And the door was open.
