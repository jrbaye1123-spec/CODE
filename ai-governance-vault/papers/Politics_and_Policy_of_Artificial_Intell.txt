EDITORIAL

Politics and policy of Artificial Intelligence
Abstract
While recent discussions about Artificial Intelligence (AI) as one of the most powerful technologies of our times tend to portray it as a predominantly technical issue, it also has major social,
political and cultural implications. So far these have been mostly studied from ethical, legal and
economic perspectives, while politics and policy have received less attention. To address this
gap, this special issue brings together nine research articles to advance the studies of politics
and policy of AI by identifying emerging themes and setting out future research agenda. Diverse
but complementary contributions in this special issue speak to five overarching themes: understanding the AI as co-shaped by technology and politics; highlighting the role of ideas in AI
politics and policy; examining the distribution of power; interrogating the relationship between
novel technology and continuity in politics and policy; and exploring interactions among developments at local, national, regional and global levels. This special issue demonstrates that AI
policy is not an apolitical field that can be dealt with just by relying on knowledge and expertise
but requires an open debate among alternative views, ideas, values and interests.

I N T RO D UCT ION : W H Y FOCU S ON POLITICS AND POLICY
O F ART I F I C IAL IN T E LLIG E N CE?
Artificial intelligence (AI) today is seen as one of the most powerful technologies. While it is often
perceived as a highly complex technical issue, AI also has major social, political and cultural implications. Over the past few years, these broader implications of AI have received considerable
attention from social scientists and humanities researchers. In particular, issues of AI ethics,
economics and regulation have been extensively studied (Floridi et al., 2018; Jobin et al., 2019;
Larsson, 2020; Schiff et al., 2021; Ulbricht & Yeung, 2022).
Recently, we are seeing growing interest in the topics of AI politics, governance and policy (af
Malmborg & Trondal, 2023; Djeffal et al., 2022; Justo-Hanani, 2022; Radu, 2021; Taeihagh, 2021;
Ulnicane et al., 2021), which initially were largely neglected. The emerging topic of the politics of
AI has been studied from a range of disciplinary perspectives that goes well beyond political science and policy research and includes contributions from disciplines and fields, such as philosophy, media studies and Science and Technology Studies (Coeckelbergh, 2022; Crawford, 2021;
Stilgoe & Mladenović, 2022). In his overview of the impacts of AI on freedom, equality, democracy and power, Mark Coeckelbergh highlights that ‘AI is political through and through’
(Coeckelbergh, 2022, p. 5). At the same time, these recent studies indicate the dearth of work
not only on the politics of AI but also on the politics of technology more broadly. Some attention
to the politics of technology has been paid in Science and Technology Studies (Jasanoff, 2016;
Winner, 1980) but political science has largely overlooked this topic.

To address this gap, this special issue brings together diverse but complementary contributions on politics and policy of AI with an aim to strengthen this topic and set out a research
agenda for the future. This special issue is a result of several years of collaborative effort and exchange of ideas. In 2020 we started to organize dedicated panels at the European Consortium for
Political Research (ECPR) General Conference. These panels at the ECPR General Conferences
from 2020 to 2022 as well as an online workshop in early 2022 helped to establish common research interests, identify synergies and create a research community. These activities led to this
special issue that includes nine articles that approach the topic of politics and policy of AI in an
interdisciplinary manner (See Table 1). In addition to political science and policy research, contributions also draw on Science and Technology Studies (Kim, 2023; Ulnicane & Aden, 2023), migration and international education research (Chou & Gomes, 2023), technology policy studies
(Schiff, 2023; Ulnicane & Aden, 2023), anthropology (Giest & Samuels, 2023) and organizational
research (af Malmborg, 2023; Erkkilä, 2023).
Articles in this issue focus on a range of aspects and approaches to study the politics and
policy of AI. While each contribution provides its own unique perspective, taken together the
studies of politics and policy of AI in this issue focus on how collective decisions and policies of
AI are made, priorities set and goals chosen in the context of plurality of actors, views, ideas and
values. That includes questions of who is included and who is excluded from making and influencing collective decisions and public policies, which roles and positions do various actors have
and which social groups are affected by adopted policies and how. These questions go straight to
the heart of the topics of distribution of power, participation and inequality. By interrogating a
diversity of views, ideas, values and actors in the politics and policy of AI, this special issue challenges a widespread view of AI as an apolitical policy field (Rönnblom et al., 2023). Contributions
in this special issue highlight that the politics of AI policy cannot just be addressed by having
more knowledge, information and evidence, but instead require an open debate of alternative
views, interests and values.
Our approach to the politics and policy of AI can be seen in the way we order articles in this
special issue. Rather than starting in a top-down manner from global politics and policy, then
moving to the regional and national level and finally finishing with effects on disadvantaged
groups, we turn this established order upside down. As we believe that a priority for politics
and policies is to challenge established power relations and hierarchies and address structural
injustices, we start this special issue with focus on the effects of AI on disadvantaged groups,
such as migrants and minorities. This includes contributions on the role of social infrastructure
for e-inclusion (Giest & Samuels, 2023), social construction of on-demand food delivery couriers (Chou & Gomes, 2023), framing of intersectional bias (Ulnicane & Aden, 2023) and gender
equality (Rönnblom et al., 2023). We then turn our attention to AI policy at national and regional
levels with studies of South Korea and France (Kim, 2023), the United States (Schiff, 2023) and
the European Commission (af Malmborg, 2023). We finish our special issue by examining developments at the global level with contributions on global standard setting (von IngerslebenSeip, 2023) and global indicators (Erkkilä, 2023).
Politics and policy of AI in this special issue is studied in diverse geographical locations, using
a range of theories, concepts, methods and data (Table 1). Contributions examine developments
in Europe, the United States and Asia-Pacific. Authors use a variety of theoretical and conceptual
frameworks, such as policy scripts, paradigms, frames and narratives, socio-technical imaginaries, social construction, policy design, multiple streams framework and game theory. They draw
on a number of research methods and data, including interviews, observations, documents analysis, comparisons and case studies.

Contributions in this special issue speak to five overarching themes (see Table 2), which are
closely intertwined with each other: understanding of AI and the concept of technology in policy
and politics; ideational and cognitive dimension of AI policy; distribution of power; relationship between change and continuity; and interactions among developments at local, national, regional and global levels. For each theme, we have formulated a set of common questions, which
are discussed below.

Artificial Intelligence
Question 1: How is AI and the concept of technology more broadly understood and defined in politics
and policy? Do instrumental understanding of technology as a tool to pursue economic and social
aims dominate in AI policy and politics? Are political, social and cultural aspects of AI and technology recognized as well? Who defines AI and technology and what are broader implications of
specific definitions and understandings?
The definition and understanding of AI is highly contested and continuously debated in research and policy (Whittaker, 2021). Such controversies surrounding the definition of AI can
be seen during the development of the EU AI Act, when the choice between broader or narrower definitions of AI became one of the hotly debated issues (Renda & Engler, 2023), resulting
in support for drawing on broad definition of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and
Development (OECD), which has defined an AI system as ‘a machine-based system that can,
for a given set of human-defined objectives, make predictions, recommendations, or decisions
influencing real or virtual environments’ (OECD, 2019, p. 2).
These debates illustrate an important theme of this special issue, namely, that AI as any
technology is not just a neutral tool but is co-shaped and co-produced by society, politics, economics, power relations and various interests (Jasanoff, 2016; Winner, 1980). Articles in this
special issue demonstrate how this co-shaping takes place at various contexts and levels from
on-demand food platforms, their couriers, regulators and consumers (Chou & Gomes, 2023)
to global rankings providing scripts for AI policy (Erkkilä, 2023) and global standard setting
where states with very diverse political and cultural traditions collaborate and compete in
deciding rules for the development and use of AI (von Ingersleben-Seip, 2023). Contributions
also show that this co-shaping is particularly important, as AI with its new applications developing fast can be seen as an emerging technology (Ulnicane & Aden, 2023) characterized by
high uncertainty and novelty (af Malmborg, 2023). However, not everyone has equal opportunities to participate in co-shaping AI and it can lead to reproducing existing inequalities and
exclusion of disadvantaged groups (Chou & Gomes, 2023; Giest & Samuels, 2023; Rönnblom
et al., 2023; Ulnicane & Aden, 2023).
AI shares many similar features with other past and present emerging technologies, such
as nanotechnology, genome editing or quantum computing. Emerging technologies are characterized by novelty, uncertainty and fast growth (Rotolo et al., 2015) as well as expectations
and hypes and their performative function (Van Lente et al., 2013). As such they need specific
policies and governance that is tentative and provides room for experimentation (Kuhlmann
et al., 2019; Ulnicane et al., 2021). However, AI today is a somewhat unusual emerging technology. As Jongheon Kim (2023) reminds in this special issue, AI has gone through several hype

Ideational and cognitive dimensions
Question 2: What is the role of ideational and cognitive dimension of AI policy? What do ideational
approaches, such as socio-technical imaginaries, narratives, discourses, frames and policy scripts,
reveal about AI politics?
Several contributions examine the role of ideas in shaping AI politics and policies through
conceptual lenses of policy paradigms (Schiff, 2023), narratives (af Malmborg, 2023), sociotechnical imaginaries (Kim, 2023) and policy frames (Erkkilä, 2023; Ulnicane & Aden, 2023).
Ideas can be powerful because they influence the definition and understanding of what the
problem is, what actions are deemed possible in addressing it and which issues are neglected
(Chou & Gomes, 2023; Rönnblom et al., 2023; Ulnicane & Aden, 2023). They also influence how
different social groups are framed and constructed in policy. Meng-Hsuan Chou and Catherine
Gomes (2023) demonstrate how food couriers are socially constructed as ‘deserving’ or ‘undeserving’ of benefits and good working conditions.
Frans af Malmborg (af Malmborg, 2023) highlights the fundamental role that narratives play
in constructing policy within uncertain contexts. In his study of the European Commission's policy response to AI as a fast-paced emerging technology, he shows how the European Commission
uses different types of narratives—sensemaking, agency construction and anchoring—to construct the new area of AI policy. Kim (2023) demonstrates how socio-technical imaginaries of AI
essentialism with their focus on dialectics of optimism and pessimism, inevitability and epochalism have influenced national AI strategies of Korea and France and had concrete consequences
leading to changes in government investment and education.
Several contributions examine competing ideas in AI policy. Inga Ulnicane and Aini
Aden (2023) demonstrate how alternative technical and social frames of bias compete for legitimacy, attention and resources. Daniel Schiff analyses the US AI policy drawing on traditional
and transformative technology policy paradigms. The traditional paradigm focuses on strategic
economic and national competitiveness objectives and technical expertise, but the transformative
one prioritizes societal and ethical concerns and the need for diverse and inclusive participation
(Schiff, 2023). While his findings reveal the presence of both paradigms in the policy documents,
the traditional paradigm still dominates as social and ethical concerns related to the transformative paradigm are subsumed into arguments favoring economic and geopolitical goals.

Representations and framings of policy problems function as devices for constructing issues
of concern (cf. Bacchi, 1999). But such representations of problems and their framing also tend to
rule out policy alternatives (Erkkilä, 2023; Rönnblom et al., 2023). Malin Rönnblom et al. (2023)
demonstrate how the Swedish policies that focus on AI and gender equality tend to represent
problem as a question of lack of knowledge and information and in that way to rule out other
possible problem representations, such as the lack of women in the development of AI systems,
or that AI systems in themselves, are outside of democratic control. Tero Erkkilä's (2023) analysis
shows how numerical and conceptual objectification in the production of AI rankings works to
delimit policy alternatives. While some policy frames delimit and rule out alternatives, others
can expand and offer alternative representations of problems and possible actions. Ulnicane and
Aden (2023) demonstrate how social frame broadens the understanding of bias in AI beyond the
traditional technical frame.

Power
Question 3: What manifestations of power do we encounter in AI politics and policies? How is power
distributed among different actors, such as the government, companies and the public or employers and employees? How does power relate to intersectional characteristics, such as race, gender or
socio-economic status?
Power is becoming one of the key topics in debates about politics and policy of AI. Highly
unequal distribution of power in AI with huge concentration of resources in a small number of
big technology companies is seen as a major problem (Ulnicane et al., 2021; Whittaker, 2021).
In this context, it becomes difficult to disentangle market and public interests (Radu, 2021). To
counteract the dominance of big business interests, a number of governments aim to undertake
more active roles in regulating and steering AI and creating multi-stakeholder forums, which
however tend to get captured by more resourceful and better organized vested interests (Djeffal
et al., 2022; Ulnicane et al., 2021). Several approaches aim to resist and challenge established
hierarchies of dominance of big companies in AI development, which particularly harm marginalized groups. Critical, intersectional, feminist and decolonial approaches argue for the need for
systemic restructuring that would enable more democratic, equal and diverse development of AI
(Png, 2022; Toupin, 2023).
Contributions in this special issue reveal complex ways in which AI reconfigures power relationships. Chou and Gomes (2023) in their work on exploitative working conditions of food
couriers demonstrate how algorithms simultaneously empower and disempower precarious
platform workers by giving them the illusion of opportunity and control, while at the same time
exploiting them. Unfair labour conditions also lead to resistance movements and techniques as
well as solidarities among exploited workers who help each other out and share information and
expertise.
The structural aspects of power also are present in the analysis of certain user groups' disadvantages and limited autonomy in accessing digital services. Sarah Giest and Annemarie
Samuels (2023) address administrative burden of algorithmic governance. Their contribution explores structural power aspects from the perspective of administrative burden. Using interviews
and observation, the article analyses library programmes as potential social infrastructure for
e-inclusion, allowing individuals to gain agency as service users and citizens.

approaches to the study of narratives often tend to delimit their ideational power by depicting
them as tools that actors deploy strategically. Instead, he explores the possibility that narratives
function on a more fundamental level as constructing heuristics framing interests rather than
being put to use within predefined interest spheres. That is particularly relevant for issues perceived as uncertain and novel—like AI policy in recent years, where actors do not necessarily
already have clear interests and preferences. In similar fashion, the existing metrics and indicator
data used in global rankings is habitually used by the novel AI readiness rankings, effectively
reproducing the existing policy scripts of competitiveness and innovation (Erkkilä, 2023).
Ulnicane and Aden (2023) in their work on framing bias in AI policy indicate how an alternative framing can challenge powerful interests. Examining the framing of bias, they show how
technological and social frames of intersectional bias in AI policy include power aspects and
suggest alternative solutions. An influential technical frame has tended to dominate discussions
of bias in AI, suggesting AI as a technological fix for bias. However, it is being challenged by an
alternative social frame that presents bias as a complex multi-faceted political, historical and
social problem, which AI exacerbates by amplifying racial, gender and socio-demographic inequalities. This latter frame considerably expands the understanding of bias in AI and the range
of possible actions to tackle it.

Change and continuity
Question 4: In a fast-developing area of AI policy and politics, what is the relationship between
change and continuity? Do novel ideas emerge or do we see strong path-dependences of established
discourses?
Several contributions in this special issue reveal that new AI policies and applications do
not necessarily lead to major changes but rather demonstrate considerable continuity and path
dependence (Mahoney & Thelen, 2010) of policy ideas, institutional responses and established
power relations (Chou & Gomes, 2023; Kim, 2023; Schiff, 2023; Ulnicane & Aden, 2023). It is
remarkable that novelty and uncertainty of AI often goes hand in hand with strong conformity
in AI policies, where past experiences but also problems are being reproduced (Erkkilä, 2023;
Rönnblom et al., 2023). However, such reproduction of existing practices calls for critical analysis
of the constitutive effects of algorithmic governance. This implies elements of both structural
and discursive constraints and path dependencies, but also drivers of change. In fact, several
contributions of the special issue highlight an interplay of structural and ideational aspects of AI
policy, marking openings for both continuity and change.

Schiff's analysis reveals that the nascent US AI policy agenda exhibits strong path-dependence

of politics, gender equality is disregarded in national AI policy that represents AI as a genderneutral policy field. At the same time, in specific policy documents on AI and gender equality the
main problem is framed as lack of knowledge and information, which is a recurring problem in
national gender equality policy.
Erkkilä's (2023) article uses network visualization and conceptual analysis of global rankings
and indicator data to show how the metrics of AI readiness have come to adopt the standard
ideas of economic competitiveness. There is a strong path dependence in AI readiness indices
that largely copy concepts, methods and data from previous rankings of economic competitiveness and innovation instead of providing a novel alternative. This delimits alternative policy
frames, such as AI ethics.

Local, national, regional and global dynamics
Question 5: How do intertwined local, national, regional and global processes play out in the field
of AI politics and policy? Do we see policy learning and convergence or do diversity and divergence
across national and regional policies dominate? How do dynamics of global cooperation and competition play out in AI politics and policy?
The politics and policy of AI are unfolding in a multi-level governance system where actions
and ideas at local, national, regional and international contexts are closely intertwined. Studies
have revealed policy learning, mutual influence and convergence across different countries, organizations and regions, while also indicating diversity in understanding and implementing specific policy priorities and instruments (af Malmborg & Trondal, 2023; Png, 2022; Radu, 2021;
Ulnicane et al., 2021).
Contributions in this special issue focus on politics and policy at different levels including
local (Giest & Samuels, 2023), local and national (Chou & Gomes, 2023), national (Kim, 2023;
Rönnblom et al., 2023; Schiff, 2023), regional (af Malmborg, 2023), global (Erkkilä, 2023; von
Ingersleben-Seip, 2023) and a mix of different levels (Ulnicane & Aden, 2023). They illustrate
complex relationships between unique local and national contexts and overarching regional and

US approach to technology with global implications.
AI policy has become a domain of global competition, where countries and regions are pitted
against each other in trying to harness its full potential (Erkkilä, 2023). However, in attempting to
gain competitive advantage over their perceived competitors, governments adopt similar traveling discourses and imaginaries of AI (Kim, 2023) and tend to privilege economic and geopolitical
problem framings over social and ethical concerns (Schiff, 2023), while future oriented narratives
are used to contain uncertainty (af Malmborg, 2023).
Importantly, global competition in AI goes hand in hand with attempts for global collaboration. The article by Nora von Ingersleben-Seip (2023) uses a game theoretical approach to explain
patterns of collaboration and competition in AI standard setting, arguing that the interplay of
actors, issues, and games determine prospects for cooperation on global AI standards within
international organizations and transnational standard-setting organizations. Her study reveals
striking patterns of technical experts hailing from geopolitical rivals, such as the United States
and China, readily collaborating on technical AI standards, whereas governments are much less
willing to collaborate on global ethical AI standards within international organizations.

F UT U RE RE SE ARCH AG E N DA ON POLITICS AND POLICY
O F ART I F I C IAL IN T E LLIG E N CE
Contributions in this special issue indicate a number of important research avenues for future
work on the politics and policy of AI. For future analysis, the issues of framing and narration
will be important, as it is through these means that problems related to AI technologies get considered for political action or not. Moreover, the temporal elements of AI policy are particularly
pronounced, as there is a strong anticipatory element in the current AI policies and narratives
on technological change. What kind of future is being portrayed and to what extent is it open for
alternatives?
Politics and policy of AI is a moving target, which is co-shaped by technological and political
developments. Several contributions examine in depth the content and adoption of AI policy
documents, guidelines and standards (af Malmborg, 2023; Erkkilä, 2023; Kim, 2023; Schiff, 2023;
Ulnicane & Aden, 2023; von Ingersleben-Seip, 2023). In future, it will be important to turn the
focus to their implementation. Articles in this issue indicate that important sites of AI politics
and policy can be found at all levels from local and national to regional and global. Moreover,
developments at one level are influenced by or tend to influence others like global developments
influencing local, national and regional ones and vice versa. More work is needed to study interactions between different levels to analyze if and how they complement or are in tension with

on and advancing existing concepts, approaches and capacities toward expanding and enriching

O UT LO O K : TOWAR D POLIT ICS A ND POLICY OF
EM E RG I NG TECH OF 2033
Ten years ago, in 2013 Review of Policy Research examined socio-political aspects of nanotechnology as one of the main emerging technologies of the time (Guston, 2013). Today in 2023 we analyze the politics and policy of AI, which is seen as one of the most powerful technologies of our
times. In the context of current hype surrounding AI, attention to nanotechnology has almost
disappeared from the political agenda. What will be the emerging technologies that will be prioritized in ten years' time? Will AI still be the main rapidly-developing technology in 2033 at the
centre of the political and research agenda? Will AI by that time govern us, write research articles
and run scientific journals? The key political question here is—if that is the type of future that
diverse social groups want. An important underlying message from contributions in this special
issue is that technologies and politics should not be taken as given and that it is important to have
opportunities for various stakeholders to meaningfully participate in co-shaping and exploring
alternative political and technological futures.
